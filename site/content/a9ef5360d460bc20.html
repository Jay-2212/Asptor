What Happens to a Woman's Property After Marriage?

• THE CARAVAN • 2026-11-13
ROMAIN DANCRE / UNSPLASH

The plot was always supposed to be hers. Her father bought it in Udaipur in the late 90s and when he died, Shreya, then twenty-nine, inherited it in a partition deed that her brother signed without argument. It was one of those rare moments that went exactly as the law says it ought to. But, this was the last time anything went exactly as the law said it should

Shreya got married that winter. By spring, the rent from a small shop built in the plot was being paid into an account that she did not hold. By March, the building plan had been approved on a letter signed by her husband, and within three years, a buyer made an offer, and she was told that it would be better if the whole family sat together to discuss it.

She was never told it wasn’t going to be hers, no one said that. They only ask who is going to manage it. Once a daughter’s inheritance crosses the threshold of her marital home, who actually owns it, and who actually controls it?

Like many things in the Indian family, inherited property does not have a linear journey after a woman marries. There are five checkpoints, and ownership and control can come apart at each point.

The Paper Trail
Under Section 14 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, property a Hindu woman possesses is her absolute property. It does not become joint property with her husband if she gets married, and it does not become the property of her in-laws. And, the Supreme Court has repeated this in unusually plain language. In Maya Gopinathan v. Anoop S.B. (April, 2024), the Supreme Court reaffirmed that a woman's stridhan is her absolute property, to dispose of at her own pleasure, and that the husband has no title or independent dominion over it as an owner.

Stridhan is a specific legal category, centered on gifts to a woman before, at and after a marriage. It is not identical to inherited property. But the principle the Court draws on, that a woman;s property is her own, is the same principle that Section 14 extends to what she inherits from her parents.

The Crises of Management
A husband will be “more familiar with these things.” A father-in-law will know the local broker. A brother-in-law can “just handle it.” A power of attorney is signed, often early, “to avoid running around.” It rarely has an end date. An authority granted for convenience can easily become a habit of control. A power of attorney does not transfer ownership. But a woman who has signed one, and who has never met her own tenant, may find that the actual distance between her and her property has become very wide.
“I signed it on a Monday in June, while I made breakfast before leaving for work”, says Kavita, a thirty-eight year old working in Education in Pune. “Eight years later I realised I did not know anything about my own tenant.”
Following the Money
Rental income from a property a woman owns is, as a general rule, income that belongs to her, and income-tax law treats it that way. That means a woman is expected to report it, and pay tax on it, in her own name. Clubbing provisions, which add one spouse’s income to the other’s, apply mainly to assets a husband has transferred to his wife, and not to property she inherited from her own parents.

In reality, things happen differently. The rent goes into a joint account, or the husband’s account, or a family account used for household costs. The tax return is filed by the husband’s accountant. If the woman has a job, her own income is declared, and the rent appears, if at all, as a line she barely sees. “My CA treated it as family income for years”, says Pooja, aged forty-one, an HR consultant in Gurgaon. “I had to ask multiple times for a statement that showed the rent from my own mother’s flat in my own name.”

Can She Sell it?
Legally, yes. A woman who owns a property absolutely can sell it without her husband’s consent. A buyer’s lawyer will want her signature, not his. Socially, the answer is more complicated, and lawyers describe this familiar scene

“A woman wants to sell, let’s say, and suddenly there's a family meeting. The husband asks her if she’s sure, the in-laws ask who will invest the money, inevitably a suggestion for saving ‘for the children’ is thrown around. No one says no, they make a thousand excuses.” Sometimes a buyer, or a bank or a registrar asks for a spouse’s no objection signature. Although the law does not require it, the woman agrees to avoid delays. Over time, the exception becomes an expectation.

There is only one place where the law itself erodes a woman’s ownership, and it only becomes visible at death. Under Section 15 of the Hindu Succession Act, if a Hindu woman dies without a will and children, property she inherited will go back to her father’s heirs, and property she inherited from her husband goes back to his. The Supreme Court has described the aim as ensuring the property “goes back to the source.”

But if she dies leaving a husband or children, everything she owns, including what she inherited from her parents passes first to her husband and children. And for self-acquired property, Section 15(1)(b) places her husband’s heirs ahead of her own parents. In November, 2025 a Supreme Court bench of Justices B.V. Nagarathna and R Mahadevan heard a challenge to that provision. The court did not rule on its validity. It instead appealed to Hindu women of all ages to make wills, and directed that where a woman’s parents or their heirs claim her property after she dies without a will, the parties must try pre-litigation mediation first.

The journey of a married woman's property reveals a distinction that Indian law has largely settled and that Indian families have largely not; ownership is a legal status, control is a daily practice. Ownership is a name on a deed. Control is who answers the tenant’s phone calls, who knows the bank password, who is asked first when an offer arrives. A woman can hold the former and have none of the latter. The two tend to separate not through force, but through care. The husband who manages the rent is, in his own mind, being helpful. The father-in-law who knows a broker is saving time. Taken in confluence, they produce an owner that is not consulted. If a daughter’s education and income can be used as a reason to withhold her share of the family’s wealth, her marriage can be used as a reason to withhold control of the share she did receive. In both cases, what looks like protection can end up becoming exclusion.

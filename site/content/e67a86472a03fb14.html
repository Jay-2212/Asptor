Beyond condolences: palliative project builds network of bereavement companions

By A. Mithosh Joseph • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-09-12

When grief strikes, the first people at hand are often family members, friends, or neighbours. But knowing how to stand beside a bereaved person without adding to their distress is a skill that can be learnt.

A bereavement companionship programme (BVP) initiated by the Kozhikode-based Institute of Palliative Medicine (IPM) has trained more than 1,000 people across India and abroad to do just that.

BVP, launched during the COVID-19 pandemic, has reached learners in countries including Thailand, the Philippines, and Bangladesh. Encouraged by the response, the IPM plans to expand its death literacy initiatives and undertake more research into the changing patterns of grief and community-based bereavement support.

The two-day foundation course is conducted in partnership with the Australia-based Death Literacy Institute Mission and Mission Better Tomorrow, the global arm of the Nanma Foundation supported by UNICEF. Though participants come from different age and gender groups, middle-aged people form the majority, according to trainers.

Conceptualised at the IPM under the supervision of its founder-director Dr. K. Suresh Kumar, the programme was launched in 2019. Its training modules have since been scientifically revised to reflect changing social contexts and incorporate feedback from participants.

The programme has also begun producing trainers beyond Kozhikode. Several participants who completed the course have subsequently become master trainers in their respective regions, helping widen the network and encourage more people, including youngsters, to acquire skills in supporting those dealing with loss.

For some participants, the training has proved useful in situations where families have been left struggling to cope with sudden deaths. Trained volunteers have supported families of accident victims at different locations, participants say.The central idea of the programme, according to P. Abdulla, a retired teacher and one of the trainees from Vadakara, is simple: a bereaved person does not always need advice or solutions but often needs someone who can listen, understand, and remain present through a difficult period.

Saif Mohammed, course coordinator, says the IPM is planning more death literacy programmes for society and research activities as part of its community-level interventions. “We have been receiving many enquiries and a very warm response from learners since the programme was launched in 2019. Recently, we conducted a two-day workshop for a group of learners in Manipur following a request from them,” he adds.

Initially offered online, the course is now available in an offline format as well. The two-day in-house training is conducted by trained resource persons for a nominal registration fee. People aged 21 and above who are interested in supporting those experiencing bereavement can participate. A 10-member team at the IPM is currently involved in conducting the programme. The leadership team of the Death Literacy Institute Mission is also in regular contact with the IPM for coordination.

The programme’s organisers say grief itself is changing as social structures and family relationships evolve. Increasing isolation, generational differences, and difficulties in communicating openly within families can make it harder for people to cope with loss. The IPM, a World Health Organisation Collaborating Centre for Community Participation in Palliative Care and Long-term Care, sees the growing network of trained companions as a way of placing some capacity to respond to grief within the community itself.

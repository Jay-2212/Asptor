Why has the FSSAI sanctioned prosecution against Bisleri? | Explained

By Kartikey Singh • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-11-25

The story so far: On November 22, the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), the country’s apex food-safety regulator, sanctioned prosecution against packaged drinking water manufacturer Bisleri International Pvt. Ltd. after a sample of its water was found to be non-compliant with prescribed safety standards, with the regulator declaring it unsafe under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006.

The public-health stakes are significant. In 2023, bottled water was the principal source of drinking water for 15% of urban and 6% of rural households, as per Data For India. For consumers buying it at railway stations, airports, highway eateries and neighbourhood shops, a sealed bottle is often taken as an assurance of safety. Yet, the data submitted in Parliament in February 2025 revealed that 333 of 1,300 enforcement samples of packaged drinking water tested in 2023-24 failed to meet prescribed standards. Similarly, in 2022-23, 650 of 1,776 samples had been found non-compliant.

According to the FSSAI, a sample of packaged drinking water (other than mineral water) collected from Bisleri’s premises was found to be non-compliant with the prescribed limits for “bromate” and was declared unsafe under the 2006 Act. Following an appeal by the food business operator (FBO), the sample was referred to a Referral Food Laboratory for further examination, which also declared the sample unsafe, citing the detection of “polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons” (PAHs).

In its response, Bisleri has maintained that its water is safe for consumption, saying the matter concerned a batch manufactured at its Goa facility in September 2025, one of 143 manufacturing plants in its network. In a statement issued on November 23, the company said the FSSAI had collected six samples from its Goa facility on September 30, 2025. While five samples met the prescribed standards, the sixth recorded a “bromate” level of 12 micrograms per litre, against the prescribed limit of 10 micrograms per litre. Further, Bisleri said it appealed on November 22, 2025, following which the sample was sent to a referral laboratory for retesting. The laboratory, as claimed, found the bromate level within the prescribed limit but separately detected PAHs. Citing the divergent test findings, the company reportedly arranged an independent examination of the same sample at an FSSAI-approved laboratory, which, according to Bisleri, also found the bromate level within limits. The company said it submitted these results to FSSAI’s Western Regional Office on February 2, 2026, but received no further communication and understood that the matter had been closed.

Water is essential for digestion, blood circulation, temperature regulation and kidney function, but its safety cannot be judged by its appearance, taste, odour or sealed packaging alone. Contamination may be microbiological, causing illnesses such as diarrhoea, cholera and typhoid, or chemical, with some substances posing risks through prolonged exposure. In India, packaged water broadly falls into two categories: (1) “packaged drinking water”, which may be sourced from surface water, groundwater or seawater and treated through processes such as filtration, decantation, reverse osmosis (RO) and disinfection; and (2) “natural mineral water”, drawn from protected underground sources and characterised by a relatively stable mineral composition. Both must meet prescribed chemical and microbiological safety standards.

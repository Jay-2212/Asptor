Why SC issued notice to Centre and CJI over appointments of judges

• INDIAN EXPRESS EXPLAINED • 2026-11-25

The Supreme Court last week issued notice to the Union government and the Chief Justice of India (CJI) in a public interest litigation by the NGO Lok Prahari seeking directions to finalise the revised Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) for appointing judges to the Supreme Court and the High Courts.

The plea argues that the revision has remained pending since December 2015, when a Constitution Bench left the task to the Centre after striking down the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC).

Here’s what to know about the case, what the MoP is, and what happened in the 2015 case concerning the NJAC and since then.

What is the case?

A bench of Justices M M Sundresh and P B Varale issued notice to the Centre and to the Supreme Court itself. In its administrative capacity, the court must respond to the plea on MoP and also bring Collegium decisions within the Right to Information (RTI) Act. 

Justice Sundresh, who is a member of the Supreme Court Collegium, first asked the counsel if there were any objections to him hearing the case. Senior Advocate Sanjay Hegde, appearing for the petitioner, told the bench that many judges would be Collegium members and that the objection would always remain, after which the court issued notice.

Earlier, the case had first come up on November 2 before a bench of Justices P S Narasimha and Alok Aradhe. Justice Narasimha recused himself because he had earlier appeared as an Additional Solicitor General in the NJAC proceedings.

What is the Memorandum of Procedure?

The MoP is the document that sets out the steps for the appointment, elevation, and transfer of judges of the High Courts and the Supreme Court. A set of mutually agreed-upon conventions, it was first drawn up on June 30, 1999, following the Second Judges case (1993) and the Third Judges case (1998), in which the Supreme Court gave the Collegium of senior judges primacy in appointments.

The CJI initiates proposals for Supreme Court appointments, while the Chief Justice of a High Court, in consultation with the two senior-most puisne judges, initiates proposals for that High Court. The timeline and the process are laid out in the MoP. As per the Law Ministry’s responses to queries in Parliament, the process of appointment of judges continues to be governed by the 1999 MoP.

What happened in the 2015 NJAC case?

On October 16, 2015, a five-judge Constitution Bench declared the 99th Constitutional Amendment — which sought to establish the NJAC for appointing and transferring judges in India’s higher judiciary — unconstitutional, restoring the Collegium system. The NJAC would comprise a six-member panel comprising the CJI, two senior judges, the Law Minister, and two eminent persons.

In the same order, it listed the case to consider measures for improving the working of the Collegium. The court received suggestions running to about 11,500 pages, which were compiled under four heads: transparency, a Collegium secretariat, eligibility criteria, and complaints.

During the hearings, then Attorney General Mukul Rohatgi submitted that drafting the MoP was an executive function, and pointed to the Second Judges case, which had left the drafting of the MoP to the government in consultation with the CJI. The bench accepted his submission and in its order of December 16, 2015, said the government “may finalise the existing Memorandum of Procedure by supplementing it in consultation with the Chief Justice of India”, and that the CJI would decide based on the unanimous view of the Collegium.

The order also set out the factors to be taken into account: eligibility criteria such as minimum age; publication of the criteria and procedure on the websites of the courts and the Department of Justice; a procedure for minuting Collegium discussions, including dissenting opinions; a secretariat for the Supreme Court and each High Court; and a mechanism to deal with complaints against candidates. The court described these as “broad suggestions” and did not fix a time limit.

What has happened since?

Until 2017, broad consultations continued between the government and the Supreme Court. Although the fine print of the draft was never made publicly available, it was reported that a draft MoP was first sent to the judiciary in March 2016. It is learnt that the Supreme Court did not yield on two key aspects: that the Centre should have the power to reject a name on grounds of “national security”, and a proposal for a permanent secretariat for the Collegium. 

A parliamentary standing committee chaired by Anand Sharma had said in December 2016 that the lack of consensus between the executive and the judiciary on the MoP was delaying appointments. On July 11, 2017, after the Supreme Court’s judgment in the contempt case against Calcutta High Court judge C S Karnan noted the need to revisit the selection process, the government wrote to the Supreme Court’s Secretary General about improving the draft. However, there was no movement on the MoP. 

In 2023, then Union Law Minister Kiren Rijiju had written to then CJI D Y Chandrachud, suggesting that a search-cum-evaluation committee for Supreme Court judges and High Court Chief Justices include a nominee of the Centre, and that committees for High Court judges include nominees of both the Centre and the state government.

The letter raised questions about whether the government is making a fresh legislative move to replace the collegium system for appointing judges. However, within days, the government, in a written reply to the Rajya Sabha, underlined that it was only a suggestion to act as a “facilitator” and that the Collegium would retain the final decision to recommend judges. 

Once again, the issue was not brought up.

On the secretariat issue, the Supreme Court over the years seems to have accepted the suggestion. The Collegium, through successive CJIs, established a system looking at data and the performance of judges, with continuous logs being maintained. 

On the “national security veto”, although the government has not formally pressed it, there are several instances where the government has, in effect, exercised a veto or simply refused to make an appointment despite the Supreme Court Collegium reiterating its recommendation. For example, in the case of the Collegium’s recommendation that Senior Advocate Saurabh Kirpal be appointed as judge of the Delhi High Court, the government had enlisted the Research & Analysis Wing to raise questions of his homosexuality and his partner’s Swiss nationality. 

What has the Supreme Court done so far?

In October 2017, a bench of Justices A K Goel and U U Lalit sought the Centre’s response on the delay in a petition by advocate R P Luthra, observing that although no time limit had been fixed, the issue could not remain pending indefinitely. The case was later listed before a bench headed by then CJI Dipak Misra, which recalled that order.

In RTI responses to the petitioner, it is learnt that the MoP as finalised by the Collegium was sent to the government in March 2017 and that the matter was “under consideration”. The Department of Justice (under the Law Ministry) has declined other requests on the ground that the information is classified and under consideration of constitutional authorities. In February 2026, Law Minister Arjun Ram Meghwal told the Rajya Sabha that “the process to revise the MoP was under consideration and that further steps would follow once it was complete”.

What happens next?

The Centre and the Secretary General of the Supreme Court will file their responses before the matter is heard again.

The unbearable burden of freedom

By Maitreyi Misra,Anup Surendranath • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-09-25

Surinder Koli was acquitted and released from prison in November 2025 by the Supreme Court after spending close to 20 years in prison, many of which were on death row. On September 18, he was found dead. Although an autopsy is awaited, early news reports described it as a suicide.

Accused of the infamous Nithari cases, at the time of his arrest in 2006 and over the next many years, Koli not only bore the brunt of the criminal justice system but also of wild public allegations. Referred to as ‘the butcher of Nithari’ and publicly accused of cannibalism, the media dredged up an image of Koli that became a frequent source of material fuelling the ‘true crime’ genre. Based on snippets from his ‘confessions’ (later found to be given under torture), the public discourse against Koli was so totalising as to leave no space for uncertainty.

The cases unravelled

Between 2009 and 2022, Koli was found guilty and sentenced to death by the trial court in 13 cases. One by one, the cases against him unravelled, as court after court found that there was no real evidence against him. In several cases, the Allahabad High Court characterised the investigation as botched up and an “easy course of implicating a poor servant of the house by demonising him, without taking due care of probing more serious aspects.” When acquitting him in 2025 in the last case, the Supreme Court made it clear that the High Court’s comments could not be dismissed as a ‘rhetorical excess’ and that the evidentiary basis for Koli’s conviction was unreliable, inadmissible and in violation of his rights. Yet, Koli came close to being executed in 2014. A stay on execution, issued by the Supreme Court just four hours before,  averted that fate.

We mention these facts not to relitigate his case, but to remind ourselves of the mourning we owe Koli. He was publicly demonised, and so a public mourning. We remind ourselves of these facts because they also raise a question. What does a justice system owe to a person it has wrongly taken so much from?

For a person like Koli, whose name still conjured a particular and monstrous image, rebuilding his life posed additional challenges. According to news reports, Koli had adopted a different name to secure housing and employment. This may have been because of the stigma associated with his name and his fear of the public’s reaction. Even after being acquitted, he was condemned by the same society he had to re-enter. The seriously botched investigation, however, has little bearing on public memory.

A structural failure

Koli’s case is not a one-off. India has a serious wrongful convictions problem. An analysis by The Square Circle Clinic of 25 years of data on death sentences shows that 40% of death sentences imposed by trial courts ultimately result in acquittals by appellate courts. That is a staggering number, made worse by the fact that individuals are made to live under a death sentence even when their convictions are barely sustainable. Once set at liberty, neither the State nor society provides them with adequate support to return and reintegrate, even as we may have subjected them to the full force of our vengeance.

There have been a few instances where the Supreme Court has explicitly recognised such travesties. It has awarded compensation in a few cases and, in 2025, recognised the need for a legislative response to wrongful convictions. But this barely skims the surface of a deep structural problem. As institutions, our prisons are far from being effective in preparing individuals for reintegration into society. Asking a prison to be interested in a category of prisoners the justice system has declared to be executed is being naive about the political and structural reality of our prisons.

These failures, which begin inside prisons, are only compounded outside. Prisoners, particularly those like Koli, who are released after spending decades incarcerated, have to readjust to a world vastly different from the one they entered when they went to prison. This readjustment involves unlearning, relearning, and learning new things that a person, now much older and unaccustomed to such a stimulating environment, has to undergo. Life has to begin anew with livelihood and housing to be found, and relationships to be re-established. The immense cognitive, emotional, psychological, and physical reorientation required is difficult to imagine.

Beyond acquittal

The eagerness driving our collective retributive instinct appears missing when we are faced with the consequences of that bloodlust. The response to such injustice cannot be ad hoc sympathy but must be structural and underpinned by principles of integrity, accountability and remediation. The integrity of a justice system lies in a meaningful acknowledgement of wrongdoing when the wrong occurs. Psychosocial support to cope with the realities of their new world healthily; safe halfway homes which allow the person to slowly adjust to a constantly changing world; reconnection with the family that they left behind; and monetary support and employment schemes to allow them a chance at independent livelihood must be seen as an inalienable part of a harsh criminal justice system, and at minimum when it exercises its coercive powers wrongfully.

Over the course of 13 acquittals, Koli lost close to 20 years of his life to a justice system that never once acknowledged the harm it caused him. It is unlikely that the injustice to Koli will become the moral reckoning it ought to be. Absent any introspection, we condemn ourselves to see many Kolis.

(The authors are with The Square Circle Clinic, NALSAR University of Law. Views expressed are personal)

Tribal children in Keralam await mentors in schools amid legal battle

By Navamy Sudhish • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-09-12

As the academic year enters its fourth month, a crucial presence is still missing in many tribal classrooms. Children from ultra-marginalised communities like the Adiya, Paniya, Kattunayakan, Urali, and Cholanaykkan arrive at school eager to learn, only to find themselves stranded in a system where no one speaks their language or understands their culture. As administrative bottlenecks and legal battles stall the appointment of Gothrabandhu mentor-teachers, these children are left without the comforting hand that guides them from home to school, resulting in growing fear, alienation, and heartbreaking dropouts.

Launched in the 2017-18 academic year, the Gothrabandhu project was specifically conceptualised to curb dropout rates among tribal students in Keralam. However, the recruitment process for mentor-teacher positions is currently stalled following a stay order issued by the High Court.

The legal challenge was initiated by former mentor-teachers who filed a petition after the government decided to discontinue their annual renewals and launch a fresh recruitment drive.

According to officials with the Scheduled Tribes Development department, their contracts had been renewed year after year for the past nine years.  “Although the initiative was originally designed to recruit educated youth from specific local communities as mentor-teachers in lower primary schools heavily populated by tribal children, several marginalised tribes ended up with minimal representation. A fresh selection drive was launched to correct this imbalance,” says an official.

‘Long overdue’

Community leaders point out that this administrative reform was long overdue, arguing that socio-economically advanced groups have long monopolised positions meant for the most vulnerable.

Pointing to a glaring systemic flaw, Manikandan C. from Wayanad’s Paniya community says out of 241 openings in schools mostly attended by Paniya children, posts had gone to Kurchiya and Kuruma community members who lack any cultural or linguistic ties to the students.

Mr. Manikandan adds that while numerous qualified youth from the Paniya, Adiya, Cholanaykkan, and Kattunayakan communities holding TTC, D.El.Ed, and B.Ed degrees remain jobless or toil in coffee and arecanut plantations, individuals from socio-economically advanced groups continue to snap up these teaching positions.

“During peak agricultural seasons children routinely drop out to join their families in wage labour. In the fields, they often encounter unemployed graduates from the community working alongside them, reinforcing a disheartening message about the value of education,” he says.

Specific dialect

“Each tribe has a specific dialect that blends elements of Kannada, Tulu, Tamil, and Malayalam,” says Chitra Nilambur, State president of the Adivasi Aikya Vedi. She says that past appointees in Wayanad and Malappuram knew only plain Malayalam and lacked any awareness of diverse tribal customs.

“How can someone who doesn’t know an Adiya child’s native tongue or a Kattunayakan child’s cultural background hope to address their learning difficulties or keep them engaged in school?” she asks.

She points out that this bureaucratic deadlock, along with socio-economic hardships, has triggered a wave of dropouts. “In the past, there weren’t many qualified candidates from these vulnerable communities, but the situation has changed entirely,” she says.

“Since these mentors only guide children up to Class IV, even candidates who have cleared Plus Two are fully equipped for the role. When qualified members of these eligible communities are barred from these positions, the foundational promise of equity slips further out of our reach,” Ms. Chitra adds.

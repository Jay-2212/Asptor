Two farmers were caught growing ganja. Why the one who harvested crop faces tougher charge

On November 17, separate police parties raided two farms in villages 12 km apart in Gujarat’s Rajkot district, following tip-offs that cannabis plants were being grown there.

Growing cannabis without a licence is illegal in Gujarat, and yet some farmers take the risk.

The crop is usually harvested in November and December, keeping the police on alert. The two farmers were booked under different provisions of the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act. Here’s why.

The seizures

A team from Vinchhiya police station in rural Rajkot raided a farm in Kharachiya Jas village and found seven cannabis plants growing amid cotton on a 2.5-bigha plot belonging to 40-year-old Paba Magan Panchala. The police uprooted the plants and seized 14.45 kg of cannabis valued at Rs 7.225 lakh. They completed the seizure by evening.

About 12 km to the west of Kharachiya Jas, the Special Operations Group (SOG) of the Rajkot Rural Police raided a farm in Chhasiya village of Vinchhiya taluka at 3 pm the same day. But the police completed their panchnama only 15 hours later, at dawn on November 18, after weighing the ganja from plants that 55-year-old Savshi Bachu Mithapara had already harvested on his five-bigha plot of land. The total seizure amounted to 705.49 kg, valued at a whopping Rs 3.52 crore.

Mithapara, who had harvested the cannabis crop just four days before the police raid, was booked for possession of ganja, an offence that carries a maximum prison term of 20 years for commercial quantities. Panchala, meanwhile, was booked for cultivating cannabis, which carries a maximum sentence of 10 years, regardless of the number of plants involved.

Why the difference of a decade?

The FIRs in both cases were filed at the same Vinchhiya police station.

Paba Magan Panchala, whose cannabis plants were still standing during the police raid, was booked under Sections 8(b) and 20(a) of the NDPS Act, which prohibit and penalise the cultivation of any cannabis plant. The maximum punishment is 10 years and a fine of up to Rs 1 lakh, regardless of whether the accused cultivated one plant or an entire field.

By contrast, Savshi Bachu Mithapara, who had already harvested his crop, was booked for possession of ganja under Sections 8(c) and 20(b) of the NDPS Act. These provisions cover the production, manufacture, possession, sale, purchase, transport and use of cannabis, among other activities.

Marijuana, or weed, known as ganja in India, consists of the flowering or fruiting tops of the cannabis plant. Under the NDPS Act, seeds and leaves are excluded from this definition when they are not accompanied by these tops.

Since the quantity seized from Mithapara’s field exceeded 20 kg, the threshold for a ‘commercial quantity’ of ganja, the punishment prescribed under Section 20(b)(ii)(C) is rigorous imprisonment of at least 10 years, which may extend to 20 years, along with a fine ranging from Rs 1 lakh to Rs 2 lakh.

Why the condition of the crop matters

There is no difference between the prescribed street value of cannabis plants and ganja decided by the Government of India. According to an October 24, 2025, notification issued by the Union Ministry of Home Affairs, all forms of ganja are valued at Rs 50,000 per kg. This is a fivefold increase from the earlier valuation of Rs 10,000 per kg, police officials confirmed.

Asked why Mithapara had been booked under the more stringent provisions of the NDPS Act despite having cultivated the crop, Inspector Fatehsingh Pargi of the Rajkot Rural SOG told The Indian Express, “The accused Mithapara had cut down the plants and set them out to dry. The number of plants could not even be counted because they had been completely dismantled branches, root and stem. So, our case is not that of cultivation of cannabis but falls in the definition of possession of ready product known as ganja.”

Inspector AC Damor of Vinchhiya police station similarly said that Panchala had been booked for cultivation because the police found standing cannabis plants in his field, rather than a harvested product.

When is cannabis cultivation legal?

The NDPS Act generally prohibits the unauthorised cultivation of cannabis plants as well as the production, manufacture, possession, sale, purchase, transport and consumption of narcotic drugs. However, there are exceptions for cannabis, with state-level rules allowing licensed cultivation for scientific or medicinal purposes.

Uttarakhand and Madhya Pradesh have legalised cannabis cultivation for several years. Himachal Pradesh joined them in July, permitting the controlled cultivation of cannabis for medicinal and scientific purposes.

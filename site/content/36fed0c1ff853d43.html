Rising cost of metals, falling demand spell trouble for Bidriware

By Nagesh Prabhu • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-09-20

Bidriware, the centuries-old metal craft unique to Bidar and renowned for its distinctive black-and-silver finish, is facing fresh pressure from the rising prices of silver, copper, and zinc.

Artisans and small-scale producers say the sharp increase in input costs, coupled with declining demand, is squeezing their margins and threatening the sustainability of the traditional craft.

Bidriware is made primarily from a zinc-copper alloy, while silver is used for intricate inlay work. With the prices of these metals rising sharply over the past two years, the cost of producing each piece has increased substantially.

Rising metal prices

Silver prices have risen from about ₹95,700 a kg in 2024 to ₹4 lakh in January 2026, before stabilising at around ₹2.50 lakh. Copper prices have increased from ₹750 a kg in 2024 to around ₹1,450 in 2026, while zinc prices have risen from ₹230 to around ₹400 a kg. Prices of these commodities fluctuate on a daily basis. Artisans largely source these materials from Hyderabad and Secunderabad.

During a visit to Bidar, several artisans and shopkeepers told The Hindu that they were finding it difficult to pass the entire increase in input costs on to customers, particularly in the price-sensitive domestic market. The resulting squeeze has affected both demand and their profit margins.

The characteristic deep-black lustre is produced using soil sourced from the historic Bidar Fort. | Photo Credit: K. Bhagya Prakash

Bidriware pieces typically contain about 90% zinc and 10% copper, with silver used for the intricate inlay. Craftsmen engrave delicate floral and geometric patterns on the surface and inlay silver into them. The characteristic deep-black lustre is produced using soil sourced from the historic Bidar Fort. The soil is added to a chemical solution during the final stage of the process, giving the metal its distinctive black finish.

How designs are etched

The designs are first sketched on the surface using a sharp metal stylus. The craft is used to make over 200 varieties of products, including hookahs, goblets, wall and table plates, decorative figurines, flower vases, caskets, jewellery, and table lamps.

Abdul Basith, manufacturer and owner of Bidri Craft in Bidar, said the rising price of silver was particularly difficult for artisans because even a small quantity used for inlay could substantially increase the cost of a product.

Umar Khayam, another artisan, said some producers were being forced to consider using less silver, making smaller products or adopting less elaborate designs to contain costs. Such measures, he said, could affect the traditional character and craftsmanship of Bidriware.

Corporate orders

Artisans said corporates from Bengaluru, Mumbai, and Delhi continue to place bulk orders for customised decorative pieces, particularly as gifts for weddings and festivals or for display in offices. Foreign tourists have also traditionally been an important market.

However, the number of foreign buyers visiting Bidar has declined sharply since the COVID-19 pandemic, said Abdul Bari, another artisan.

Depending on their size and quality, Bidriware products are generally priced between ₹2,500 and ₹50,000.  | Photo Credit: K. Bhagya Prakash

Depending on their size and quality, Bidriware products are generally priced between ₹2,500 and ₹50,000. Shopkeepers said their annual business has fallen from an average of about ₹50 lakh to ₹20 lakh because of declining demand and rising metal prices.

The craft is also facing a generational challenge. Artisans form a distinct community, with nearly half belonging to the older generation. | Photo Credit: K. Bhagya Prakash

The craft is also facing a generational challenge. Artisans form a distinct community, with nearly half belonging to the older generation. Younger people are reluctant to enter the profession because income is uncertain and work depends heavily on orders, which are not regular, said Mr. Basith.

Labour-intensive

The detail-intensive craft, believed to have originated during the 15th-century Bahmani Sultanate, is highly labour-intensive.

The detail-intensive craft, believed to have originated during the 15th-century Bahmani Sultanate, is highly labour-intensive. | Photo Credit: K. Bhagya Prakash

With the number of skilled craftsmen falling to fewer than 75, artisans and shop owners have urged the State government to establish a dedicated training centre to provide skill-based training to unemployed youth and help preserve the craft. | Photo Credit: K. Bhagya Prakash

Even a small vase can require up to 10–12 days of work. With the number of skilled craftsmen falling to fewer than 75, artisans and shop owners have urged the State government to establish a dedicated training centre to provide skill-based training to unemployed youth and help preserve the craft.

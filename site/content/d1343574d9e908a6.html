Leaked papers and lost years in India’s recruitment system

By Rahul Verma • THE HINDU OPINION • 2026-08-13

Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan resigned on July 24 after five weeks of protest over a leaked NEET-UG paper as part of a Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) campaign that drew a 36-day hunger strike by activist Sonam Wangchuk. The leak was not an isolated failure. However, NEET has faced leak controversies in 2021, 2024 and now 2026, following attempted leaks in 2017 and the cancellation of its predecessor exam over a leak in 2015.

This year’s cancellation and retest were followed by at least 21 student suicides. Days later, a differently structured protest erupted in Ranchi. Since July 29, aspirants have held an indefinite sit-in demanding cancellation of the 14th Jharkhand Public Service Commission preliminary exam and a CBI probe into alleged irregularities spanning seven years across JPSC and Jharkhand Staff Selection Commission examinations. Student leader Devendra Nath Mahto began an indefinite hunger strike on August 2 and agreed to take water on August 5 after Wangchuk appealed to him, making clear the wider protest would continue. The CJP has extended support to the Jharkhand agitation.

The distinction between these two protests matters. The Delhi movement had one clear trigger, the May 2026 NEET paper leak and one office that could answer for it, the Education Ministry. That case had a clear endpoint, a resignation, compensation and a reform pledge, all tied to that one Ministry. Jharkhand’s aspirants are pointing to a pattern across two agencies and seven years and, no single resignation can close a case like that, because it involves an entire recruitment system, not one office. The Delhi scandal had a clear start and end. Jharkhand’s problem has been building for seven years, with no such clean edge.

The architecture of delay

Government auditors have already documented what a multi-year recruitment failure looks like. A Comptroller and Auditor-General report on the Madhya Pradesh Public Service Commission found that selection was completed with a delay in 18 of 48 examinations reviewed, ranging from one to 25 months. In one case, the commission took 20 months just to issue an advertisement after receiving a formal requisition. Five departments took between 31 and 68 months to report 101 vacancies at all, close to six years in some cases. The commission conducted only 22 of the 44 examinations scheduled in its own calendar and held 21 examinations outside the planned timeline altogether. What Ranchi’s students are alleging in practice, official auditors have already documented on paper, an absence of binding timelines at every stage of recruitment.

The human cost shows up, partially, in the government’s own labour data. The 2025 annual report of the Periodic Labour Force Survey recorded youth unemployment among those aged 15 to 29 at 9.9%, more than three times the national rate. That figure cannot show the aspirant who cleared a preliminary exam and is waiting years for a result that may never arrive without an investigation forcing the issue. As I wrote in Frontline, such a candidate falls into an administrative limbo that official surveys fail to categorise as joblessness. A multi-year recruitment failure can persist without registering as a national statistic, unlike a single leaked paper.

The electoral cadence of recruitment

The clustering of vacancy announcements around elections is visible in the announcements themselves. Ahead of Bihar’s 2025 election, Opposition leader Tejashwi Yadav promised a government job for every household within 20 days of forming a government and rival parties made competing pledges within months of voting. Governments recruit outside election years, too, but political attention concentrates around one moment. Announcements, jobs pledges, fresh notifications, get timed for visibility before a poll, while the harder second half, issuing the exam, completing verification, finalising results, slips well past it, as the Madhya Pradesh audit shows happening over months and years without an election forcing urgency. In Uttar Pradesh, reporting has flagged recruitment delays and paper leaks as ongoing issues ahead of its 2027 election. Jharkhand goes to the polls only in late 2029 and, its government has assigned the case to its Criminal Investigation Department (CID), which has made 14 arrests and conducted raids across several districts. Protesters have rejected the CID probe as inadequate, since it is a state agency investigating a state commission and continue to demand a CBI inquiry or an independent panel of outside judges.

The CJP’s Delhi protest secured a resignation, compensation and a commitment to reform examination integrity, a resolution suited to a movement built around one exam cycle and one Ministry, even though the pattern of leaks long predates this year. Jharkhand’s aspirants want an investigation broad enough to examine an entire recruitment system across two commissions and multiple cycles. That is a slower, more contested demand, since it lacks a single trigger to rally national attention.

The accountability language that led to a resignation in July grew out of a scandal with a clear beginning and end, even though it was itself just one entry in a longer history of leaks. Ranchi’s protesters are testing whether that language can extend to a slower, audit-documented failure spanning seven years and two agencies, in which blame is distributed rather than concentrated in one office. Whether the State responds with the CID probe protesters have rejected or, with the independent investigation they are demanding, will say a great deal about whether this accountability moment reaches beyond a single Ministry.

Closing that gap requires three auditable reforms instead of election-timed promises. Every State commission should publish a binding annual examination calendar, whose absence the Madhya Pradesh audit named as a root cause of delay. Prescribed recruitment timelines should be publicly tracked against actual performance, so delays are documented instead of staying invisible. Labour force surveys should include a distinct category for candidates awaiting results beyond a stipulated timeline, so years lost to delays are measured instead of vanishing into a count never designed to capture them.

Rahul Verma is an independent researcher and sociology educator

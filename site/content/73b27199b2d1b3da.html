India’s recurring pollution crisis calls for a sustained movement for cleaner air

By Akshay Rout • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-11-23

Deepavali, the festival of lights, left a trail of dark smoke across large parts of India’s northern plains, especially around the national capital, Delhi, where 80 million people, particularly infants and the elderly, are struggling to breathe. The Air Quality Index (AQI) at several locations in the National Capital Region (NCR) had touched 400, indicating the severity of the conditions. Other cities and towns in the north, south, east and west, though better off than the NCR because of favourable weather and geographical advantages, do not have pristine air either. Air in most Indian cities contains pollutants, including particulate matter, at levels about eight times higher than the threshold set by the World Health Organization. The worst part of the problem is its dire predictability, except for concessions from the weather. The costly lessons from the capital have implications for the entire country.

The Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM) swiftly moved up the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) from Stage I to Stage III within 15 days. The Commission, a statutory body established as an emergency response to toxic air conditions, has been cracking down on farm fires in Punjab, with its teams visiting fields to check incidents of stubble burning. PAVAN-I3C, the CAQM’s new Integrated Command and Control Centre, seeks to capture non-compliance in industries, traffic and transport systems and trigger immediate action against pollution spikes. Construction and demolition (C&D) activities have been halted until the 20th of this month. Inspections of construction sites and checks of Pollution Under Control Certificates (PUCC) are also vigorous. Last week, in an unprecedented move, CAQM directed Delhi and other NCR States to plan reduction of annual pollution levels by 15-20% forthwith.

Regulatory moves, however essential, are not enough. Fines and work stoppages offer only partial deterrence, while obstructions and congestion on roads are an outright failure of enforcement. Institutional compliance, enforcement and monitoring by agencies are paramount, but civic behaviour is equally critical.

The asks of people are not any great sacrifices, but some lifestyle retuning: use public transport and minimise use of personal vehicles, work from home, avoid construction activities for a few weeks, clean air filters at homes and in vehicles, avoid activities which generate dust, heat and smoke, and avoid open burning of solid waste and biomass. It would be unimaginable in many other countries for vehicle owners to be reminded of the pollution caused by their vehicles only during the months when the air becomes unbreathable. If clean air is part of the right to life, citizens also have a charter of duties to follow.

The causes of pollution are well known. These straddle sectors, including industry, roads and buildings, vehicles, agriculture, shrinking green cover, municipal waste and urban living. However, citizens and their behaviour, in their varied roles as farmers, car and house owners, builders, contractors, and urban residents, remain at the centre. People complain and curse, and even silently suffer sickness from foul air, but sensitive and responsive behaviour remains elusive. The expansion of metro services and the availability of more public transport have not persuaded private car owners to switch. The Supreme Court went the extra mile by allowing firecrackers this Deepavali, while imposing justifiable restrictions on their toxic content and duration of use. Yet, citizens’ responses have not remotely matched the severity of the problem. It might seem contradictory, but many would like to reap all the dividends of GRAP without complying with its requirements.

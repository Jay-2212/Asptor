Winters in Punjab are usually dominated by conversations around smog and stubble-burning. This November, however, another winter phenomenon is attracting attention: early fog.

The India Meteorological Department (IMD), Chandigarh, has forecast dense fog at isolated places in Punjab, Haryana and Chandigarh from November 19 to 22, weeks before prolonged winter fog normally becomes a regular feature of the northern plains in December and January.

Why is Punjab seeing a foggy November?

Meteorologists attribute the early fog to moisture incursion, light north-westerly winds, atmospheric stability, and night-time cooling. The cyclonic system over the Bay of Bengal is also influencing the larger circulation and moisture pattern.

Fog forms when air near the ground cools to its dew point, the temperature at which moisture in the air condenses to form water droplets. Thus, adequate moisture in the lower atmosphere is as essential as cooling.

Minimum temperatures have been below normal at several locations in Punjab, though the pattern is not uniform. According to IMD Chandigarh observations on November 19, Chandigarh recorded 10.4°C (1°C below normal), Patiala 9.3°C (-2°C), Hisar 10°C (-2°C), Narnaul 9.5°C (-2°C) and Rohtak 10.6°C (-1°C).

This variation is significant because fog is localised: dense patches can form where moisture, surface cooling and weak winds coincide, even as nearby stations record normal or above-normal temperatures.

Surinder Pal, director, IMD Chandigarh, said moisture availability, weak winds and falling night temperatures were creating favourable conditions. The IMD’s November 19 bulletin also noted a weak western disturbance, with a trough (an elongated area of relatively low atmospheric pressure) in the middle layers of the atmosphere over eastern Afghanistan and north-west Pakistan.

Why can fog be dense in some areas while nearby areas are clear?

While moisture provides the essential ingredient for fog, light winds and atmospheric stability determine its formation and persistence. Surinder Pal said moisture incursion, along with light north-westerly winds, was creating favourable conditions. Weak winds restrict the mixing and dispersal of air near the surface, allowing moisture to accumulate.

As the ground loses heat through radiation at night, the air above it cools. Once it reaches saturation, water vapour condenses into droplets. Stable atmospheric conditions restrict vertical mixing, allowing radiation fog to develop and persist, particularly during the night and early morning.

Local variations in humidity, irrigation, canals, agricultural fields, vegetation and surface temperatures explain why fog can be dense in isolated locations while nearby areas remain relatively clear.

Is Cyclone Akvan also influencing fog formation in north India?

The Severe Cyclonic Storm Akvan is another factor influencing the prevailing weather pattern. Surinder Pal pointed to its influence on moisture incursion. Cyclonic systems can alter atmospheric circulation and moisture movement over large areas, although Akvan should not be considered the sole or direct cause of fog in Punjab and Haryana.

Is this fog the same as the smog Punjab usually witnesses in November?

No. Fog and smog are different atmospheric phenomena, although they can occur together.
Fog consists of tiny liquid water droplets suspended near the ground, while smog is polluted air containing particulate matter and gases from vehicles, industries and stubble burning. Weak winds and atmospheric stability can trap pollution near the surface, worsening air quality and visibility.

Punjab has frequently witnessed early November smog in recent years, with stubble-burning emissions adding to other sources of pollution. This November, however, the early fog is primarily linked to meteorological conditions, rather than being simply another episode of pollution-induced haze.

The two can coexist. Aerosol particles in polluted air act as cloud condensation nuclei, providing surfaces for water vapour to condense and potentially influencing fog formation and visibility, experts said.
Pal said the early fog does not necessarily signal the start of the prolonged winter fog season. It reflects a temporary convergence of favourable weather conditions, while more persistent fog episodes are usually witnessed in December and January.

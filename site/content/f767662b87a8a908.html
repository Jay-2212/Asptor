SC upholds NCLAT order to revisit ₹2,962-cr. Videocon resolution plan

By The Hindu Bureau • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-11-17

The Supreme Court on Monday (November 16, 2026) upheld the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal’s (NCLAT) decision to send Twinstar Technologies’ ₹2,962-crore resolution plan for 13 Videocon group companies back to lenders for reconsideration, holding that the plan failed to comply with mandatory provisions of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016, governing payments to dissenting financial creditors.

A Bench of Justices P.S. Narasimha and Alok Aradhe dismissed appeals filed by Twinstar challenging the appellate tribunal’s January 2022 order. The Bench also affirmed the NCLAT’s authority to remit an approved resolution plan to the CoC, while delineating the circumstances in which such power may be exercised.

“Remand must rest on a finding of legal non-compliance, whether under Section 30(2) or under any other law within the meaning of Section 61(3)(i),” the Bench observed, adding that the power must be exercised “sparingly and with circumspection”. It further held that any order of remand must be accompanied by strict directions to adhere to the timelines prescribed under the IBC and the relevant regulations.

The Bench held that while the commercial wisdom of the CoC must ordinarily be respected, neither the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) nor the NCLAT could rewrite or modify a resolution plan to rectify statutory deficiencies. Where a plan failed to comply with the law, the appropriate course was to remit it to the CoC for reconsideration.

The dispute arose from insolvency proceedings against Videocon Industries Limited and 12 other group companies, whose admitted claims amounted to approximately ₹64,839 crore. Twinstar’s resolution plan, approved by the CoC in December 2020 with 95.09% of the voting share, proposed a financial outlay of ₹2,962.02 crore. The NCLT, Mumbai, approved the plan on June 8, 2021, despite noting that it entailed a haircut of 95.85%, with creditors facing a substantial reduction in their admitted claims.

Under the proposed repayment structure, financial creditors were to receive ₹200 crore upfront, alongside non-convertible debentures worth ₹2,700 crore carrying an annual coupon of 6.65%. The debentures were to be redeemed in instalments beginning 25 months after the closing date and extending up to the sixth year.

Three dissenting financial creditors — Bank of Maharashtra, IFCI Limited and the Small Industries Development Bank of India (SIDBI) — challenged the approval before the NCLAT, questioning the proposed distribution of proceeds and the treatment of creditors who had voted against the plan. They also pointed to discrepancies between the amounts reflected in the resolution professional’s compliance certificate and the distribution calculations circulated to lenders.

On January 5, 2022, the NCLAT set aside the approval and referred the matter back to the CoC, holding that the proposed repayment through deferred debentures did not satisfy the statutory requirement of payment to dissenting financial creditors. It also found that the NCLT had effectively modified the resolution plan by directing upfront cash payments to these creditors, a decision that fell within the exclusive commercial domain of the CoC.

Affirming this reasoning, the top court held that dissenting financial creditors could not be compelled to accept securities in place of monetary payments. It also found that the plan failed to ensure the statutory priority accorded to such creditors over those who had voted in its favour.

“A general stipulation of compliance with the law cannot be a substitute for a specific provision in the plan as to how, when and in what form the dissenting creditors will be paid,” the Bench said.

The court also considered whether the CoC could revisit a resolution plan after approving it. During the appellate proceedings, creditors holding 94.98% of the voting share sought reconsideration of the plan following observations made by the NCLT and the NCLAT.

The Bench held that the CoC does not become functus officio merely upon approving a resolution plan. Since the approval had been challenged and stayed by the NCLAT, the CoC was entitled to reconsider its decision, it said.

“The CoC is enjoined by the Code to choose the best resolution plan from the market and provide for any measure as part of such plan to ensure sustained life of the corporate debtor,” the Bench said.

The Bench, however, cautioned against repeated remands, observing that they carried “a clear cost” in the form of prolonged litigation, erosion of certainty, increased financial burden on the parties and depletion of asset value.

“The power of remand must, therefore, be exercised in a manner that secures its benefit by incurring the least potential costs,” the Bench said.

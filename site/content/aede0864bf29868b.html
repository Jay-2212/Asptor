Ex-officials say Census data must be confidential amid concerns over potential NPR, NRC links

By Vijaita Singh • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-09-10

The Union government’s decision to advance the Population Enumeration phase of Census 2027 in four poll-bound States, coupled with the introduction of several new questions in the Census schedule, has prompted some former Census officials to raise concerns regarding the quality of data that is to be collected and the potential links between the Census, the National Population Register (NPR) and, ultimately, the National Register of Citizens (NRC).

There is a precedent for Census data having been used to prepare the NRC. Records show that this took place in 1951, during Independent India’s first Census, when the NRC was prepared across the country by "copying" data from the Census schedules. However, for unspecified reasons, a countrywide NRC was never published, except in undivided Assam, which at the time also included Manipur and Tripura.

Speaking to The Hindu, former Deputy Registrar General of India K. Narayanan Unni stressed that any link between the Census and population-registration activities remains speculative. However, some of the information being sought in the latest Census questionnaire resembled data collected during previous NPR updation exercises.

The NPR, first created in 2011 and initially proposed to be updated with the first phase of the now-scrapped Census 2021, has been put on the back burner due to stiff opposition from political parties and civil society groups, who had expressed concern that the data could be used to create a countrywide NRC amid allegations that the process could lead to a large number of people being left out of citizenship.

Mr. Unni said, “NPR is generated under the Citizenship Act, and data is supposed to be published. However, Census data is confidential and can only be used for statistical purposes. But in 1951, Census data was used to prepare the NRC in Assam...” He added that even though Census data is confidential, this doesn’t mean that the 1951 exercise of using it to prepare NRC won’t be repeated.

On August 14, 2026, the Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India (RG&CCI) notified a set of 40 questions to be asked during the Population Enumeration phase, or the second phase, of the Census, adding 13 new questions or data fields that were not part of the 2011 Census questionnaire.

Some of the additional questions, such as particulars of a person’s parents, including their names, religion, place of birth, Aadhaar number, mobile phone number, voter ID and driving licence details, mirror those asked in the rehearsal form of the NPR in 2019.

The NPR had been updated in 2015 and already has the family-wise database of 119 crore residents.

The 1951 exercise

The 1951 Census report by R.B. Vaghaiwalla, the Superintendent of Census Operations for Assam, Manipur and Tripura, shows that the NRC was conceived primarily as a Census and administrative tool rather than a citizenship-verification exercise.

“An important innovation of this Census was the preparation of the NRC, in which all important Census data was transcribed from the Census slips. The Register uses the same symbols and abbreviations and is a copy of the Census slips,” the 1951 report said.

Missing NRC entries were copied from Census slips, and missing Census slips were recreated from NRC entries under a special reconciliation exercise undertaken by Vaghaiwalla. This took place even though there had been countrywide instructions from the Registrar General to limit such checks “in the interest of economy of time and money”.

According to Vaghaiwalla, the register was intended to be maintained permanently and updated by village officials, thereby providing continuity between Censuses. Though Section 15 of the Census Act, 1948 ensures confidentiality and says that “records of census [are] not open to inspection nor admissible in evidence”, the NRC was prepared using the data, establishing a precedent of using Census data to prepare the register.

A former Census official, speaking on the condition of anonymity, said the NPR and the Census were legally distinct exercises governed by different laws. The official pointed out that the updation of the NRC in Assam in 2019 was not based on NPR data and required a separate data collection exercise.

Undercounting concerns

On September 5, the RG&CCI advanced the Population Enumeration phase of Census 2027 in the poll-bound States of Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Uttarakhand and Goa, while deferring the exercise in violence-hit Manipur. The Census will also be the first in Independent India to enumerate castes beyond Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

Mr. Unni said that the decision to conduct the Census in four States ahead of the rest of the country could lead to under-enumeration of migrant workers, particularly from Uttar Pradesh.

“Census dates were traditionally fixed around March 1 because migration was relatively low during that period. Advancing the exercise in States that are due to hold Assembly elections may mean that several migrant workers return home to vote and are not available where they ordinarily reside. By the time they reach their home State, enumeration there may already be over, creating the possibility that some migrants are not counted at either place," he said.

Other than these four States, as well as Ladakh and snow-bound areas of Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Jammu and Kashmir, population enumeration in the rest of the country will take place in February 2027 with a reference date of March 1, 2027. Snow-bound areas have traditionally been counted before the rest of the country.

India follows a de facto system of enumeration, under which a person is counted where he or she is present during the Census period. Mr. Unni said that even a small increase in undercounting could affect data quality because past Censuses have already recorded some degree of under-enumeration.

On the government’s decision to defer the Census in Manipur, he said that conducting the enumeration without public cooperation would compromise data quality. However, he argued that postponement could deprive the State of demographic evidence relevant to claims about migration and population change.

‘Respondent fatigue’

Mr. Unni questioned the utility of some of the new questions, particularly those relating to identity documents and vaccination history. “Many of these details are already available in administrative databases. It is not clear what statistical purpose some of these questions will serve,” he said.

He also cautioned that a lengthy questionnaire could increase “respondent fatigue” and create practical difficulties for enumerators, most of whom are government school teachers.

“If a household is required to answer dozens of questions for every family member, it will increase the time taken per household and may lead to resistance, particularly in urban areas where enumerators are often unknown to residents,” he said.

The other former Census official raised questions over the inclusion of Aadhaar details in Census 2027, noting that the collection of Aadhaar numbers may require changes to existing legal provisions.

Need for moderation in public speech

• THE HINDU OPINION • 2026-07-29

Mark Twain once said, “the difference between the almost right word and the right word is really a large matter.”

In recent times, public discourse in Tamil Nadu’s political arena has come under scrutiny. Concerns are being raised across sections of society about the tone, content, and quality of speeches delivered by leaders from both the ruling and Opposition parties. Allegations, counter-allegations, legal actions, agitated debates on television channels and newspapers have become frequent, indicating not merely isolated incidents, but a worrying trend.

Political speech is not just a tool for mobilisation; it is a reflection of democratic culture. Furthermore, it is one of the most basic tools and features of democracy. It is how parties take their policies and views to the people. However, when language descends into personal attacks, provocation, or sensationalism, it sets a precedent. And a bad one at that.

History teaches us that while such precedents are easy to establish, they are exceedingly difficult to reverse.

Global precedents

The experience of the United States during the McCarthy era in the 1950s offers a telling lesson. Senator Joseph McCarthy’s speeches, marked by sweeping allegations and dramatic accusations, created a climate of fear and mistrust. Careers were ruined, institutions were strained, and public discourse suffered.

It took a moment of collective introspection — captured in the famous question, “Have you no sense of decency, sir?”— to begin restoring balance. The damage, however, lingered far beyond that moment.

Similarly, in Europe, the final years of the Weimar Republic saw a steady decline in the quality of political speech. Increasingly harsh and polarising rhetoric eroded mutual respect among political actors and weakened democratic norms. What began as verbal aggression eventually contributed to a breakdown of stability itself.

The lesson was clear: words, when used unchecked, can shape outcomes far beyond immediate political gains.

Leaders shape discourse

In a mature democracy, citizens expect more from their leaders. They look for clarity of vision rather than clever invective; for solutions rather than slogans; and for dignity even in disagreement.

Political speech must inform, inspire, and unify — not inflame or divide. The divisive and exclusive, as opposed to inclusive, trends in speeches are causing deep concerns at the local as well as the global level.

Moderation in speech is not a sign of weakness; it is a mark of strength and responsibility. It reflects confidence in one’s ideas and respect for the audience. When there is solid substance, the words spoken become the natural foundation on which to reach the people.

Leaders at all levels set the tone for public behaviour. When they uphold dignity, society follows. When they abandon it, the consequences ripple outward to the last man.

At this juncture, there is an opportunity for all stakeholders —across party lines— to pause and reflect. Reaffirming norms of respectful discourse, setting internal standards for communication, and prioritising public interest over momentary applause can help restore balance. Media needs to play a crucial role by not promoting or repeating sensational utterances for increasing their viewership.

For the next generation

Democracy thrives not merely on the freedom of speech, but on the wisdom with which that freedom is exercised. The younger generation, the future of the country, must be inspired by principled political discourse. When leaders speak with integrity and purpose, they not only shape public opinion and culture but also motivate young citizens to strive for excellence and grow into worthy contributors to the nation.

The need of the hour therefore is, not louder voices, but moderated ones — grounded in dignity, guided by responsibility, and committed to the larger good.

The author is a former IAS officer of the Tamil Nadu cadre.

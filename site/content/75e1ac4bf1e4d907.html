NEET-2027: What reforms could be proposed, what more needs to be done

Recurring controversies over the National Eligibility-cum-Entrance Test (NEET) have exposed persistent vulnerabilities in India’s public examination system. The NEET-2024 examination, which saw paper leaks, exam malpractices, and candidates getting abnormal scores, led to the constitution of the Radhakrishnan Committee — whose 101 recommendations pertained to exam reforms.

Yet, the NEET-2026 examination in May was marked by allegations of paper leaks and cancellations, and the subsequent retest (Re-NEET) in June was followed by widespread student protests. Repeated and incorrect questions as well as answer-key discrepancies have further undermined confidence in competitive examinations.

In July 2026, the government constituted a Nandan Nilekani-led High-Powered Task Force (HPTF) to recommend comprehensive reforms in public examinations, including those conducted by the National Testing Agency (NTA). Its initial deadline of October 2026 for submitting its report has been extended to January 2027 after receiving over 50,000 suggestions and extensive stakeholder consultations.

With no interim report publicly available, NEET-2027 candidates face considerable uncertainty. With computer-based testing (CBT), a secure question vault, last-minute paper assembly, and digital delivery among the reported proposals, we look at what reforms are likely to be considered, their limitations and challenges, and what needs to be done to restore fairness and institutional accountability beyond NEET-2027.

Why have earlier reforms failed to prevent recurring NEET crises?

Following the NEET-2024 controversy, the Dr Radhakrishnan Committee recommended reforms covering examination security, technology, infrastructure and institutional processes.

But the NEET-2026 crisis raises questions about their implementation and oversight. Vulnerabilities exist across the examination chain — from question setting and review to transportation, storage, examination-centre management, and evaluation. Allegations of insider involvement make these particularly serious.

New recommendations will have little value unless earlier failures are investigated, institutional responsibility is established, and proposed reforms are rigorously piloted.

Is CBT feasible for NEET?

CBT reduces dependence on physical printing, packaging, transportation, and storage, while enabling computerised administration, controlled access, and automated evaluation. But it introduces digital vulnerabilities, system failures, and cybersecurity risks.

A nationwide examination requires adequate centres, secure systems, reliable power and connectivity, trained personnel, and contingency arrangements. Uneven infrastructure and varying computer familiarity among candidates also raise concerns about accessibility and fairness.

CBT’s feasibility must be established through infrastructure assessment, large-scale pilots, and independent technical audits. Changing the examination format alone cannot guarantee security or fairness.

Can a question vault and last-minute digital delivery secure the question paper?

Security must begin with question creation and validation, not merely paper transportation. Question setting, review, syllabus alignment, translation, and answer verification are all potential points of compromise, particularly with concentrated access.

A proposed question vault would maintain a large pool of independently vetted questions. The final paper could be assembled shortly before the examination, encrypted and digitally transmitted to centers, thereby reducing advance access and dependence on physical transport.

The question paper repository, selection software, servers, encryption keys, and transmission networks require restricted access, traceable operations, and independent cybersecurity audits. Ultimately, security depends on the entire chain — from question setters and validators to final selection and delivery.

How can fairness be ensured across multiple CBT sessions?

Multiple NEET sessions require different question papers, yet all candidates must compete on a common merit list. Ensuring equivalent difficulty is therefore a major challenge.

Statistical normalisation, potentially using percentile scores, may help compare performance. But its reliability depends on the comparability of the papers and on statistical assumptions. Even small adjustments can affect ranks and admission prospects.

Through rigorous pilots and independent validation, NTA must establish that papers have comparable syllabus coverage, difficulty, and discriminatory power. The methodology and its limitations must be transparent and auditable. Normalisation cannot compensate for fundamentally unequal question papers.

Beyond technical reforms, changes to eligibility, examination schedules, sessions, and format could further reduce vulnerabilities. Such changes require advance notification and phased implementation. In the absence of timely official announcements, major structural changes to NEET-2027 should be ruled out.

Can a two-stage NEET examination address normalisation concerns?

Story continues below this ad

A two-stage examination, on the lines of IIT-JEE (Mains and Advanced), is one possible alternative. All eligible candidates would appear in a screening examination (Mains), followed by a final examination for shortlisted candidates (Advanced) based on predefined cut-offs or a specified number of qualifiers. A single-session final examination does not need normalisation.

This model raises questions about shortlisting, eligibility, syllabus, question paper format and difficulty, scoring, and cut-offs. It would increase examination overheads but improve fairness in rankings for top scorers.

Should Class XII marks carry weightage in NEET? Can NEET be conducted twice a year?

On Tuesday (November 17), The Indian Express reported that providing weightage to Class 12 board exam scores in NEET-UG has emerged as a key proposal from several states during “consultative meetings” held so far with the Nilekani-led task force. Another proposal discussed was offering more than one attempt in a year.

Weightage to Class XII marks could recognise sustained academic performance, but variations in board examinations and marking standards make equitable weightage difficult. Conducting NEET twice a year could offer candidates another opportunity to improve their scores, but would increase logistical costs and raise concerns about normalisation and admission timelines.

A two-stage NEET, along the lines of IIT-JEE, could address both concerns: Class XII marks could be used in determining eligibility for the second-stage single-session examination.

Could the HPTF recommend changes to eligibility, attempts, or age limits?

With over two million candidates competing for limited medical seats, NEET’s unlimited attempts and the absence of an upper age limit raise questions about the examination’s capacity and the size of the candidate pool. Candidates can appear repeatedly, irrespective of age, even beyond 60.

Age limits and attempt restrictions, common in most competitive examinations, could help narrow the candidate pool.

Can GenAI improve question generation and the quality of examinations?

Conventional software can select pre-vetted questions from a secure vault without GenAI. GenAI could assist in question generation, syllabus mapping, translation, preparing answer options, and calibrating difficulty, creating a larger, more diverse question pool.

AI-generated questions, however, may contain factual errors, ambiguities, incorrect answer keys, syllabus errors, or translation defects. Expert validation is essential; AI may assist question development.

What should the HPTF deliver to restore confidence in public examinations?

Story continues below this ad

The Nilekani-led HPTF must provide a time-bound implementation roadmap that defines institutional responsibilities, resources, technical safeguards, independent oversight, and accountability. Major reforms must undergo large-scale pilots before nationwide implementation.

NTA also needs permanent expertise in examination management, cybersecurity, psychometrics, digital forensics, and vigilance. The HPTF’s success must ultimately be measured by whether examinations become secure, transparent, reliable, and fair. India needs not merely new examination technology but also trustworthy examination standard operating procedures and institutions.

From Gram Swaraj to SVAMITVA: unlocking the mystery of capital in Madhya Pradesh

By Nidhi Nivedita • THE HINDU OPINION • 2026-10-03

Early one morning, in the small town of Sheopur Kalan, Sitaram opened the wooden door of his house and paused for a moment in the courtyard. There was the old neem tree under which his father had spent most afternoons. There was the small room where his son now studied. At the back was the cattle shed. His wife had placed a tulsi plant against the wall.

Sitaram had lived there all his life. His father had lived there before him. For him, there was no question about whom the house belonged to. It was his ancestral home. But that morning, he needed something more from it. His son was about to begin a new course. Sitaram needed about three lakh rupees. Someone in the village told him, “Why don’t you go to the bank? You have a house. You have land.”

So he went. The bank manager looked at his papers. “Your name is in the revenue record?” “Yes.”

“Do you have the map?” “Yes.”

“The property is in the abadi?” “Yes.”

The manager examined the papers carefully. “We will need to verify the property and the documents required for creating the appropriate security.”

Sitaram nodded. He wanted to say: The whole village knows this is my house. But he understood that a modern bank needed something more than familiarity. It needed a reliable, verifiable representation of the property and the right attached to it. He returned home that afternoon thinking about something he had never thought about before: What does it really mean to own a house?

India has been answering versions of that question for centuries. The history of Indian land administration is, in many ways, a history of making land identifiable. From the measurement and revenue arrangements recorded in the Ain-i-Akbari to the elaborate surveys, cadastral maps, and settlement records developed during the colonial period, the State built systems to answer basic questions: What land exists? Where is it? Who has rights over it?

Yet another landscape existed within the revenue map. Villages were not merely fields. They were houses, courtyards, cattle sheds, lanes, and community spaces — the lived geography of rural India. In many parts of the country, the inhabited settlement came to be described administratively as the abadi.

This landscape was often socially certain but institutionally less visible. Everyone might know whose house it was. But could its boundaries be precisely established? Could the right be recorded in a standardised form? Could a bank, an heir or another institution confidently identify the property?

This distinction lies at the heart of Hernando de Soto’s The Mystery of Capital. The Peruvian economist argued that assets acquire greater economic potential when ownership is represented in a form that institutions can recognise, verify and transact upon.

A house may be valuable to a family and known to every neighbour. Yet if the right attached to it is difficult to establish or transact upon, much of its economic potential remains locked within the informal system. De Soto described such assets as a form of “dead capital”.

The insight is particularly relevant to rural property. The challenge is not simply to establish that a house exists. It is to create an institutional representation of the property that can travel beyond the village — to a bank, a public authority, a lawful transaction, an inheritance or a dispute-resolution process.

This is the larger significance of SVAMITVA.

Three stages to transformation

The drone survey is its most visible feature, but the drone is only the beginning. The larger objective is to create clearer spatial and documentary records of rural residential property, reduce disputes, and enable property rights to participate more effectively in the formal economy. The transformation is, therefore, not merely from an unmapped house to a mapped house. For analytical purposes, there are three stages to this transformation. First, physical possession: “This is my house.” Second, administrative recognition: “The State recognises my right in this property.” Third, transactional visibility: “A bank, purchaser, heir, registrar or another legitimate institution can reliably understand and deal with this right.” It is this third layer that Madhya Pradesh is seeking to strengthen.

The State’s Swamitva Adhikar Abhilekh Nishpadan evam Panjeeyan Yojana seeks to take eligible property rights, established and recorded through the SVAMITVA process, towards an appropriate institutional expression through execution and registration, in accordance with the applicable legal framework.

The significance of this step lies not merely in registration as a procedure. It lies in reducing the distance between a right that has been established and a citizen who needs that right to be recognised by institutions beyond the village.

For lakhs of rural families, the State’s decision to bear the applicable stamp-duty and registration-related financial burden is particularly significant. A cost that may appear modest in administrative terms can be a meaningful barrier for a household with limited disposable income. Removing that burden makes formalisation more accessible. The effort to take the process closer to citizens through village-level arrangements is equally important. A reform of this scale should not require the rural citizen to navigate a maze of offices and procedures merely to obtain the institutional expression of a right that has already passed through the survey and record-making process. Thus, the benefit is not only financial. It is also procedural and institutional. The State is seeking to reduce the transaction costs of formalisation — the cost of money, time, information, and administrative navigation. And that brings us to the question: What does this have to do with Mahatma Gandhi’s idea of Gram Swaraj?

At its heart, Gram Swaraj was a vision of villages possessing the capacity to understand their resources, manage their affairs and participate in their own development. The village of the 21st century is very different from Gandhiji’s village. Its citizens use banks and smartphones, access digital services, migrate for work. and participate in markets far beyond the village boundary.

Uncertain physical reality

Yet the principle remains: a village cannot plan as effectively when its physical reality is uncertain. Citizens may not be able to fully realise the institutional and economic value of their property when their rights are difficult for institutions to verify. In this sense, good property records can be the infrastructure of Gram Swaraj in the 21st century.

Gandhiji spoke of empowering the village. De Soto spoke of unlocking the economic potential of property through formal recognition. SVAMITVA brings these ideas into the age of digital governance — connecting the citizen, the village, and the State through a clearer representation of property.

Now we return to Sitaram. A few weeks later, he stood again in the courtyard. The neem tree was where it had always been. His son’s books lay scattered in the small room. The tulsi plant still stood against the wall. Somewhere at the back, the cattle shifted in the shed. Nothing had changed. And yet, something had. The house now had a clearer place on the map. Its boundaries had a more precise spatial identity. The State had a clearer record of the right that Sitaram’s family had understood for generations.

Sitaram closes the wooden door that evening with contentment. And perhaps this is where Gandhiji’s talisman offers its simplest test: does the system work for the person who has the least power to navigate it?

For Sitaram, the answer is simple. Yes.

The house has not changed. Its place in the world has.

The author is Principal Revenue Commissioner and Commissioner, Land Records, Government of Madhya Pradesh

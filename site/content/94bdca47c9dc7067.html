After 13 years of waiting for a game that managed to spawn a generation of memes about milestones that were achieved before its release, Grand Theft Auto (GTA) VI was launched worldwide on Thursday (November 19).

The game is the latest instalment of a nearly 30-year-old franchise that began as a crime-themed video game and evolved into a sprawling satire of American society. Created in Scotland, the franchise has since become one of the most recognisable names in gaming.

On Friday, Rockstar Games said GTA VI had generated $3.79 billion in retail sales on its first day, setting what it described as a record for an entertainment launch across any medium. The publisher said 32.5 million copies had been sold ahead of the release.

The latest release centres on two new characters, Jason and Lucia, a couple whose lives revolve around petty crime in a fictionalised version of Florida. But early reviews suggest that GTA VI is also departing from some of the conventions that made its predecessors famous, placing greater emphasis on relationships and making players confront the consequences of their actions.

The changes raise an interesting question about a franchise long associated with violence, controversy and the freedom to cause chaos: how did GTA become such a defining part of video game culture, and what does its latest evolution mean for the legacy it has built?

From cops and robbers to a virtual world

Grand Theft Auto debuted in 1997 as a straightforward game that granted players the freedom to steal cars, commit crimes and complete missions. Players viewed the action from overhead, navigating fictional American cities while completing missions.

“Rockstar Games’ ambition with Grand Theft Auto was to essentially prove that the interactive medium can be just as compelling and gripping as film and television,” Sujay Nellore, a long-time GTA fan who has played the series since its debut in 1997, told The Indian Express. “It started small, but they have iterated and innovated with every single mainline entry into the franchise.”

The turning point came with GTA III, released in 2001. Set in Liberty City, a fictionalised version of New York City, the game cemented the franchise’s visual identity. It introduced the distinctive logo typography borrowed from the TV show The Price Is Right, along with the illustrated, checkerboard-style cover art that subsequent instalments retained.

The game moved the action into a fully three-dimensional environment. Instead of viewing the city from overhead, players could follow their character at street level, exploring the city and completing missions in a more immersive world. It introduced more cinematic storytelling, with voice acting by Hollywood actors Michael Madsen, Kyle MacLachlan and Frank Vincent.

Game designer turned academic Bob de Schutter told The Indian Express that GTA III put the genre of open-world games into the spotlight. “Its structure of story missions spread across a massive city is still the template for open-world games. It also made strides in storytelling, bringing many movie tropes into games,” he added.

The game’s influence extended beyond Rockstar. Former PlayStation Studios chief Shuhei Yoshida told The Game Business podcast that the success of GTA III, Vice City and San Andreas influenced the direction of several Sony-owned studios. Naughty Dog, Insomniac Games and Sucker Punch, which had previously developed games featuring cartoonish characters, subsequently moved towards more mature, realistic action-adventure titles, including Uncharted, Resistance: Fall of Man and Infamous.

For Nellore, the franchise’s evolution went beyond improvements in graphics and gameplay. “At its core, GTA retains this to this day, but now we have richly detailed worlds within which your actions as a player make sense. You now have player characters that have strong motivations, things they want to do, lines they won’t cross,” he said.

GTA: Vice City, released in 2002, was a cinematic gangster fantasy set in a fictionalised Miami, drawing heavily on Scarface and Miami Vice. GTA: San Andreas, released in 2004, featured a larger world comprising fictionalised versions of Los Angeles, San Francisco and Las Vegas, while exploring gang culture and social divisions.

GTA’s America: satire and the American Dream

As the worlds grew more elaborate, so did the stories Rockstar sought to tell. Nellore points to GTA IV (2008), whose protagonist, Niko Bellic, is a veteran of the Yugoslav wars struggling with his past and his pursuit of a better life in America.

The game took a decidedly political tone, trading the bright, neon-lit settings of earlier instalments for a greyer, more sombre world that highlighted the debt and hardships faced by an immigrant in the US. Released in April 2008, as the US was entering a recession, it also gave its protagonist a more complex personal history. GTA IV was arguably Rockstar’s most ambitious release yet.

“Rockstar saw the blending of narrative and gameplay and made GTA IV, with leading man Niko Bellic, who was their best-written character to date,” Nellore said.

The American Dream has remained a recurring theme across the franchise. John Wills, professor of American Studies at the University of Kent, told The Indian Express that GTA represented a case of the “tourist gaze”, calling it an outsider exploration of what America symbolises. “Rockstar’s work is like a 21st-century version of Alexis de Tocqueville, a French philosopher, visiting America in the 1830s and writing about it: an outsider’s lens on the American experiment,” he said.

In GTA V (2013), Rockstar explored the contradictions of the American Dream through three protagonists, including Michael, a wealthy but disillusioned former criminal, and Franklin, a young Black man seeking to escape poverty. The game also satirised American consumerism, corporate culture and political institutions through its fictional advertisements, radio programmes and missions.

Wills points to billboards advertising ammunition or promising poor service on domestic flights, Michael’s counselling sessions in which he expresses disillusionment with the American Dream, and missions that involve attacking American technology businesses. The satire, he argues, is embedded in practically every aspect of the game world.

Unlike television satire, players actively participate in Rockstar’s fictional society. “We are active in the satirical playground – we experience it and even shape it. We are part of Rockstar’s message,” Wills told The Indian Express.

How GTA has remained relevant for 13 years

Despite the 13-year gap between GTA V and GTA VI, the franchise never really disappeared. A major reason was GTA Online, the multiplayer component of GTA V, which allowed players to explore Los Santos together, undertake missions and stage elaborate heists.

For Nellore, this was key to the game’s longevity. “What really blew up was GTA Online, which brought the central feature of GTA V – the Michael Mann movie-style heists – into a cooperative multiplayer setting where you could team up with your friends and go on heists together,” he said.

Rockstar continued to introduce new missions, activities and other content over the years. Meanwhile, players created their own characters and stories through role-playing communities, attracting audiences on YouTube and Twitch. In 2023, Rockstar acquired Cfx.re, the team behind FiveM, which enabled customised GTA V multiplayer servers.

Nellore, who has played GTA since the late 1990s, said the franchise’s audience had also changed. While older players such as himself preferred its single-player stories, newer audiences increasingly sought multiplayer experiences.

The commercial success of GTA V and GTA Online also gave Rockstar the financial freedom to invest enormous resources in its next instalment. As Nellore put it, “For a studio that is as detail-oriented, as dedicated to ensuring a coherent open world, as strict as ensuring their writing is top-notch, that means they can take their time with the product.”

How GTA VI is challenging the franchise’s conventions

Over the years, the franchise has attracted sustained criticism for its violence and portrayal of women, particularly sex workers. Its games allowed players to attack police officers and innocent bystanders, while critics accused Rockstar of glamourising criminal behaviour.

“The GTA franchise is primarily a male fantasy. Women are in the games to be seen and interacted with through a male gaze. And Rockstar has often courted controversy by deliberately including strip clubs and objectifying women,” Wills said.

The satire itself was not without contradictions. As Wills has argued in his research, the games frequently reward players for pursuing wealth and violence, even while their stories criticise the excesses of American society.

The controversies also contributed to the franchise’s notoriety. “It’s the video game equivalent of slapping a parental advisory sticker on a rap album: all those stickers did was make these albums more sought after,” Nellore said.

Early reviews, however, suggest that GTA VI is challenging some of the conventions the franchise has long been known for.

The game introduces a more responsive and realistic world. Players can still steal cars, commit crimes and cause chaos, but evading the police has become more difficult, with surveillance systems and more sophisticated police responses. Everyday activities have also become part of the experience, from maintaining vehicles and decorating homes to scuba diving and birdwatching, Reuters reported on Saturday.

For Nellore, the change reflects how the franchise has matured alongside its audience. “GTA as a series has grown up with the people who played it. What used to be the thoughtless act of committing a crime in, say, GTA 3 has now been replaced by the game’s systems encouraging you to think about your actions before you commit the crime,” he said.

He sees the increased friction in gameplay as the franchise’s biggest innovation, making players more conscious of the decisions they take as Jason and Lucia.

The changes also extend to how players interact with the protagonists. Players can choose to spend time with Lucia and Jason as a couple, holding hands, going on dates or looking after each other. Reviews suggest that neglecting the relationship can affect how the characters interact and influence the story itself.

Rockstar’s developers have also described the relationship as central to the game’s storytelling. In an interview with Game Informer, the studio explained how allowing Jason and Lucia to interact outside scripted missions gave players greater influence over their relationship.

Wills, who had not yet played the game at the time of this conversation, viewed the introduction of a female protagonist as having “threatened one of the foundations of GTA as a ‘man’s world’.”

“Depending on how the game/story plays out, this could be a very welcome shift in GTA towards inclusivity and a breakthrough title for the company. But more likely, there will be overtones of welcome progress and some interesting character development, but players will still be able to find at least some of the old Rockstar sexualised content along the way.”

There is also a tension between greater realism and the freedom that has long defined GTA. De Schutter pointed out that allowing players to decide when to advance the story makes it difficult for developers to build narrative tension in the way a film editor can.

Wills raised a similar concern about GTA VI. He compared its increasing emphasis on everyday activities with Rockstar’s Red Dead Redemption 2, which introduced elements such as personal hygiene and maintaining a camp into its depiction of frontier life.

“However, the more real a game gets, maybe the sense of the game as fun and escape decreases. That is a danger,” he said.

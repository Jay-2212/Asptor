In Jensen Huang’s return, unanswered questions of national security, governance and politics

Less than two weeks after he stepped down as the chief executive of Nvidia amid revelations of an affair with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset, Jensen Huang is back at the helm of the world’s most consequential artificial intelligence (AI) chipmaker. His dramatic reinstatement on Monday (November 23) has triggered a euphoric response on Wall Street, but a bipartisan backlash in Washington.

While Huang’s return has brought an end to an unprecedented leadership crisis at Nvidia, several of the questions that precipitated his departure remain unanswered – from the extent of the potential national security risks posed by his relationship to the standards of corporate governance at the chipmaker, and the propriety of the White House’s intervention even as multiple investigations remain underway.

At the heart of the controversy is also a larger tension, as to whether the enormous economic and strategic significance of Nvidia, whose chips underpin much of the global AI industry, has complicated the ability of American institutions to hold its leadership accountable.

Nvidia’s shares rose 31.5% on Monday, adding roughly $950 billion to its market capitalisation. But even as investors celebrated Huang’s return, lawmakers from both the Republican and Democratic parties questioned the circumstances under which the company’s board reversed its earlier decision.

The national security question remains unresolved

Huang’s troubles stemmed from his relationship with Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman formerly associated with Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that has been under scrutiny over the suspected diversion of advanced Nvidia chips to China. US intelligence officials reportedly suspect her of having worked for Chinese intelligence, an allegation Beijing has denied.

The concerns are particularly significant given Washington’s attempts to restrict Beijing’s access to advanced American semiconductors, which can be used for developing sophisticated AI systems and have potential military applications.

In announcing Huang’s reinstatement, Nvidia’s board said it had received inputs from senior officials in the US government and was confident that his return posed no national security risk. However, it neither identified these officials nor disclosed the nature of the information they had provided.

Crucially, Nvidia said its own investigation, being conducted by a committee of independent directors with the assistance of external legal counsel, would continue. Huang has committed to cooperating with the investigation.

Separately, on the same day that Huang returned, the Trump administration moved to add Megaspeed and several associated entities to its trade blacklist, restricting their access to sensitive American technology.

In a joint statement last week, the US Departments of Justice and Commerce said Nvidia had stopped selling chips to Megaspeed in June 2025. Investigations into the suspected diversion of chips remain underway. No evidence establishing Huang’s personal involvement in espionage or illegal chip shipments has been made public.

The board’s U-turn raises questions of corporate governance

As recently as November 17, Nvidia’s board chairman Harvey Jones had categorically ruled out Huang’s return as CEO, telling investors during the company’s earnings call that the executive’s personal conduct was inconsistent with the chipmaker’s standards and values.

Six days later, the board unanimously voted to reinstate him, saying that following several meetings with Huang, it was satisfied with his forthrightness and ability to lead the company. However, it offered little explanation about how the concerns over his personal conduct had been addressed.

Apart from his relationship with Alice Huang, the Nvidia co-founder reportedly had affairs with two employees of the chipmaker, including Kimberly Powell, its vice president of healthcare, according to Reuters. Huang has publicly acknowledged marital infidelity but has not individually addressed all the reported relationships. He has since also admitted to having affairs with more women than previously reported.

These revelations raise questions separate from those concerning national security, including about workplace power dynamics, potential conflicts of interest and the board’s oversight of its chief executive. They also give a rare insight into how Nvidia operated, and how the cult of power surrounding Huang – paired with the chipmaker’s astronomical rise in fortunes – have effectively made him and the company too big to fail.

Trump’s intervention and questions of conflict of interest

In an extraordinary instance of a sitting US President intervening in the leadership affairs of a publicly listed company, Donald Trump acknowledged on Monday that he had personally persuaded Nvidia’s board to bring Huang back.

“And I convinced them. I made the deal,” Trump told CNBC.

Trump also claimed that US Attorney General Todd Blanche had confirmed that the government did not possess evidence of unlawful conduct by Huang. However, the absence of evidence of criminal wrongdoing does not necessarily address the broader concerns about potential national security vulnerabilities.

There is also a financial dimension to Trump’s intervention. According to a Reuters analysis of his financial disclosures, the President owns Nvidia shares worth between $11 million and $55 million. Monday’s rally could have increased his net worth by as much as $17 million.

The White House has maintained that Trump’s investments were independently managed and that neither the President nor his family could influence investment decisions. Trump’s intervention is particularly also consequential given Huang’s sustained lobbying of the US administration to ease restrictions on the sale of AI chips to China, an important market for Nvidia.

A bipartisan backlash, and more investigations

Huang’s reinstatement has drawn criticism even from within Trump’s Republican Party.

Republican Senator John Curtis questioned which government officials had advised Nvidia’s board, what information they had provided, and how the company had arrived at its assessment while its independent investigation remained incomplete.

“Nvidia’s board says it relied on input from senior US officials to conclude that bringing Jensen Huang back posed no national security risk. I have some questions. Who in our government weighed in? What did they tell the board? And on what basis did the board conclude there was no risk?” Curtis wrote in a social media post on X. “Last week, the board said it didn’t intend to reinstate him. I’d like to know what changed.”

Republican Senators Pete Ricketts and Kevin Cramer also criticised the decision, with the latter arguing that while Nvidia’s board would bear the legal and reputational risks of bringing Huang back, any national security consequences would extend to American citizens and the military.

Democratic Senator Elizabeth Warren called the decision reckless and demanded that the Trump administration explain its role to Congress. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer promised investigations into any wrongdoing by the administration and Nvidia executives.

Separately, California Attorney General Rob Bonta has issued an investigative subpoena to Nvidia to ascertain whether the company or Huang’s actions enabled China to obtain advanced chips or confidential information, and whether the chipmaker misled investors about such risks.

The controversy is unlikely to die down anytime soon, particularly with Democrats set to take control of Congress in January. Huang is scheduled to appear before lawmakers for closed-door questioning on December 1, followed by a public hearing before the Senate Banking Committee on December 3.

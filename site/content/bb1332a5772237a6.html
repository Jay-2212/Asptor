Memory in the pocket

By Sharada A.L. • THE HINDU OPINION • 2026-08-10

A friend recently forgot her phone at home. She wanted to call her family but could not because she did not remember a single phone number. For a while, she felt completely stranded. She had forgotten how to reach them, having quietly delegated that memory to the phone she had left behind.

That incident stayed with me because my own memory behaves very differently.

I remember phone numbers with surprising ease. Not because I have an exceptional memory, but because I look for patterns. Many numbers begin with familiar combinations. The remaining digits often have a rhythm of their own. Once I discover that rhythm, the number stays with me.

A language of its own

It made me realise that memory has a language of its own. It loves rhythm, pattern, and association. We remember songs because of their melody, poems because of their rhythm, stories because one event leads naturally to another. Even a phone number becomes memorable when it stops being a random string of digits and begins to make sense.

Names, however, are another matter.

More than once, I have been talking to a close friend and have suddenly forgotten her husband or children’s names. Once, I asked, “How are the kids?” She replied that they were doing well. Normally, I would have asked about each child individually. Instead, the conversation stopped there because I could not remember their names.

Finally, I admitted it. We laughed. But I came away with an uncomfortable thought.

It wasn’t just embarrassing. It restricted the conversation. I realised that forgetting names does not merely mean losing information. It means losing the ability to make conversations personal and meaningful.

When I address people by their names, they know I remember them. When I remember a story they shared months ago, they know I was listening. Memory is a language of relationships, not simply a storage device.

For years, I assumed these lapses were simply a part of growing older. Then I realised it wasn’t just me.

I see young people interrupting conversations to search for facts they probably know. Colleagues looking up phone numbers they have dialled for years. Children depending on GPS to find places they visit regularly. They forget names and details as much as I do. Perhaps this isn’t about age at all.

A little browsing revealed that researchers call this the Google effect or digital amnesia. When we know information can be retrieved instantly from a device, our brains make less effort to remember it. Gradually, we become better at remembering where information is stored than the information itself.

I am not suggesting that technology is the villain. Far from it. My phone has made life easier in countless ways. But somewhere along the way, it has quietly become an extension of my memory.

That made me ask myself a simple question. Are there some memories I should never outsource? For me, the answer is yes.

The phone numbers of the people I love. The names of people who matter. The birthdays of dear ones. The route to a friend’s house. A few poems, songs and prayers that have become a part of me. The stories that bind me to family and friends. Not because my phone cannot remember them. But because I need to remember them to reach out and relate to the people who matter to me, without the mediation of a device.

In U.P., the Congress needs the Samajwadi Party

By Syed Kamran • THE HINDU OPINION • 2026-10-01

As Uttar Pradesh heads toward its next Assembly contest, Congress strategists are dealing with an uncomfortable truth: in the current alliance, the Samajwadi Party (SP) holds the stronger hand. Beyond the optics of ‘INDIA bloc unity’, the arithmetic and organisational depth point the same way: the Congress needs the SP more than the SP needs the Congress.

The first distinction is structural. The SP is not a personality-led outfit that borrows crowds for a season and vanishes when the tide turns. It has a functioning cadre and booth-level workers; Yadav-Muslim social capital built over three decades; a growing share of non-Yadav Other Backward Classes (OBCs) and Dalits; and a district-to-village skeleton that survives bad election cycles. Compare this to the Trinamool Congress or the Aam Aadmi Party which is based on ‘leader-and-supporter’ constructs; they are formidable when the leader is popular but structurally thin once the wave recedes.

The Congress’s State unit suffers from the ailment the SP has cured. It is a supporter-driven shell, dependent on media cycles, occasional Rahul Gandhi yatras, and transient online support, lacking ground infrastructure to convert sentiment into votes on polling day. The SP’s cadre, by contrast, is a durable asset that isn’t going anywhere regardless of who wins in 2027; that permanence is the leverage.

The second argument, arguably more decisive, is vote arithmetic. U.P.’s contest is no longer a fragmented four-cornered fight; it has hardened into a bipolar contest between the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) and the SP-led opposition. The Bahujan Samaj Party’s (BSP) vote share has been steadily eroding, and what remains of it, along with residual anti-BJP sentiment scattered across smaller caste-based and regional outfits, has no natural home. It flows toward whichever non-BJP formation has the most chance of winning. 

In a bipolar contest, that residual “anyone but the BJP” vote doesn’t split evenly; it consolidates behind the party seen as the real challenger, and in U.P. that is unambiguously the SP, not the Congress. Voters unhappy with the BJP will vote strategically by gravitating to the SP because it has repeatedly shown itself as the only outfit within striking distance of the BJP’s vote share. The Congress, hovering in single digits across most U.P. assembly segments for over a decade, isn’t part of that calculation for the median opposition voter. Its vote-multiplying value is limited, while the SP’s brand pulls in the anti-incumbency vote that decides seats.

The third argument is narrative ownership. The 2027 U.P. election is shaping Akhilesh Yadav as the face of the opposition and the PDA (Pichda, Dalit, Alpsankhyak (Backward, Dalit, Minority)) as the organising social coalition. This has been Mr. Yadav’s framework since the 2022 defeat, and it has visibly reshaped how backward and Dalit voters outside the traditional SP base view the party. The Congress has no competing narrative in U.P. with similar traction; its national Nyay (justice) framework and Bharat Jodo-adjacent messaging hasn’t localised into a U.P.-specific story the way the PDA has.

When one partner owns the narrative and the other is borrowing space within it, the question of who ‘steers the wheel’ answers itself. A realistic path for the Congress lies in being a disciplined junior partner that helps consolidate the PDA coalition in seats where it adds value, such as in Brahmin and urban pockets, rather than insisting on parity in seat-sharing. Overreach risks fracturing an already-fragile anti-BJP consolidation. 

The bottom line

This does not mean that the Congress brings nothing to the table; a national party’s symbolic weight does have marginal value. But marginal value is not equal leverage in a State as diverse as U.P. The SP has cadre permanence, the bipolar arithmetic that captures residual anti-BJP votes, and narrative ownership through Mr. Yadav and the PDA. In any honest reading, this is an alliance where one partner can survive alone and the other cannot; and that imbalance should shape how the Congress negotiates seats and its place in the coalition.

The Congress’s best bet is to fight this election in alliance with the SP with realistic seat demands rather than inflated ones — concentrating resources on a limited set of seats. A good strike rate on fewer seats does two things: it hands the Congress a stronger hand in seat-sharing talks for 2029, and it gives the party a real bloc of sitting MLAs; the kind of ground presence no amount of central leadership visibility can substitute for.

Syed Kamran is an independent journalist and political commentator based in Lucknow.

From Steve Jobs to Jensen Huang, the unending cult of the American tech founder

Eleven days after he stepped down from Nvidia amid revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset, Jensen Huang has returned as the chief executive of the artificial intelligence (AI) chipmaker.

His reinstatement, following the intervention of US President Donald Trump and mounting pressure from investors, marks one of the most dramatic corporate comebacks in recent Silicon Valley history.

While the circumstances surrounding Huang’s departure and subsequent return are unusual, American technology companies have a long history of turning to their founders in times of crisis. From Apple’s Steve Jobs to OpenAI’s Sam Altman, some of the industry’s most prominent executives have returned to lead the companies they helped build, in some instances after being unceremoniously ousted by their boards.

These comebacks also reflect the peculiar relationship that Silicon Valley has with its founders, whose individual personalities, technological vision and ability to command investor confidence can become almost inseparable from the companies they run.

The success of executives such as Jobs has helped perpetuate the belief in Silicon Valley that founders possess an intuitive understanding of their businesses that professional managers cannot easily replicate. However, Huang’s comeback presents a somewhat different proposition. Unlike Jobs, who returned to an Apple facing business challenges, Huang’s reinstatement followed a crisis precipitated by revelations about his own conduct.

His return, even as investigations remain underway, also raises questions about the extent to which the commercial indispensability of a founder can supersede conventional considerations of corporate governance and accountability. There are many unanswered questions around national security, governance and political intervention surrounding Huang’s reinstatement too.

Here is a look at some of Silicon Valley’s most high-profile founder comebacks, the circumstances that led to their return, and how they shaped the fortunes of their companies.

Steve Jobs, Apple (1997)

Arguably the most famous corporate comeback in Silicon Valley’s history is that of Steve Jobs, who co-founded Apple in 1976 with Steve Wozniak.

In 1985, following a bitter power struggle with then Apple CEO John Sculley, whom Jobs himself had recruited from PepsiCo, the company’s board sided with Sculley, leading to Jobs’ departure. He went on to establish computer company NeXT and subsequently acquired the business that became animation studio Pixar.

Steve Jobs with the Macintosh computer in January 1984. He would be fired the next year. (Wikimedia Commons)

By the mid-1990s, Apple was struggling financially and losing ground to Microsoft-powered personal computers. Its acquisition of NeXT brought Jobs back into the company, and in September 1997, he became its interim CEO.

What followed was one of the most remarkable turnarounds in corporate history. Under Jobs, Apple launched the iMac, iPod, iPhone and iPad — products that fundamentally altered the consumer technology industry. By the time Jobs stepped down in August 2011, Apple had become one of the world’s most valuable companies, and continues to.

Sam Altman, OpenAI (2023)

Perhaps the closest parallel to Huang’s dramatic reinstatement is the return of Sam Altman as OpenAI’s CEO in November 2023 – sans the numerous extramarital affairs.

OpenAI’s board abruptly fired Altman in November 2023, saying that he had not been consistently forthcoming in his communications with directors. The decision came barely a year after the launch of ChatGPT, which had catapulted OpenAI to the forefront of the global AI industry.

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman. (Wikimedia Commons)

The sacking triggered a revolt within the company, with an overwhelming majority of its employees threatening to leave unless Altman was reinstated. Microsoft, OpenAI’s biggest financial backer at the time, offered to hire Altman and his colleagues.

Within days, an agreement was reached for Altman’s return, accompanied by an overhaul of OpenAI’s board. An independent review subsequently concluded that his conduct did not warrant his removal.

The episode demonstrated how even a board specifically constituted to prioritise the safe development of AI could find itself at odds with the commercial interests of investors and employees.

Michael Dell, Dell (2007)

Michael Dell, who founded his eponymous computer company in 1984, while retaining the position of chairman. His successor, Kevin Rollins, struggled with intensifying competition and slowing growth, prompting Dell’s return as CEO in January 2007.

In 2013, Dell took the company private in a $24.9-billion deal with private equity firm Silver Lake, allowing it to undertake a restructuring away from the scrutiny of public markets.

Three years later, the company acquired data storage giant EMC in a landmark technology deal, transforming Dell from a business largely associated with personal computers into a major enterprise technology and computing infrastructure company.

Jack Dorsey, Twitter (2015)

Jack Dorsey, Twitter’s co-founder and first CEO, was pushed out of the position in 2008 amid concerns over his management of the fledgling social media platform.

Jack Dorsey returned as Twitter CEO in 2015. (Wikimedia Commons)

In 2015, as Twitter struggled to expand its user base and faced pressure from investors, Dorsey returned as CEO following Dick Costolo’s resignation. For the next six years, he simultaneously ran Twitter and payments company Square.

When Dorsey stepped down again in November 2021, handing over the reins to India-born Parag Agrawal, he explicitly questioned the culture of founder-led technology companies, arguing that excessive dependence on founders could become a limitation for businesses.

Larry Page, Google (2011)

Google co-founder Larry Page relinquished the CEO position in 2001 to Eric Schmidt, an experienced technology executive brought in to manage the rapidly expanding company.

In 2015, Larry Page oversaw Google’s corporate restructuring under a new parent company, Alphabet. (Wikimedia Commons)

A decade later, Page reclaimed the position, with Schmidt becoming executive chairman. Unlike several other high-profile founder comebacks, the transition was not precipitated by an acrimonious boardroom battle.

In 2015, Page oversaw Google’s corporate restructuring under a new parent company, Alphabet, allowing its core Internet business and more experimental technological ventures to operate separately. He became Alphabet’s CEO, while Sundar Pichai took charge of Google.

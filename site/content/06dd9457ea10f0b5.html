Youth angst that should worry every political party

• THE HINDU OPINION • 2026-08-03

India is often celebrated as the world’s youngest democracy. We usually discuss that in the context of economic growth, startups, innovation and consumption. We should also discuss what it means for democracy.

Some numbers are worth pausing over. India has nearly 98 crore registered voters. Voter turnout hovers around 65–66%. The current ruling party secured 23.6 crore votes, translating to roughly 36.5% of votes polled. Now look at another set of numbers. Around 37 crore Indians are between 15 and 29 years of age. Nearly 75 crore Indians are below the age of 30.

For decades, India’s youth have largely been viewed as an economic resource. It is time political parties began viewing them as a democratic force.

The recent unrest around the NEET paper leak and the emergence of the CJP movement have triggered sharp political debates. Every political party, irrespective of ideology, has historically sought to ride public movements. Equally, many movements themselves have eventually evolved into political platforms. That is neither new nor uniquely Indian. There are already competing narratives around the antecedents and motivations of the current movement. But that isn’t the most interesting question.

The real question is this: why did it resonate? If a movement that reportedly began as online satire can bring young people onto the streets across parts of the country, it reflects something deeper than social media virality. It reflects accumulated frustration. Whether that frustration is entirely justified, partially justified or emotionally amplified is almost secondary. Public sentiment rarely explodes overnight. It accumulates.

I was recently watching the Malayalam film One, starring Mammootty. The film revolves around an idea almost unimaginable in India’s current political framework, the right to recall. The premise is simple. If more than half the voters in a constituency believe their elected representative has failed to honour the mandate, they can collectively demand a fresh election. Predictably, almost the entire political establishment opposes the proposal.

In the film, when the Chief Minister refuses to abandon the reform despite overwhelming resistance inside the Assembly, he is eventually forced to resign. The people then bring him back with an even stronger mandate since he chose accountability over political convenience. Is it utopian? Certainly. Would it survive in India’s political ecosystem? Simple answer, no. But it raises a profound question. Should accountability arrive only once every five years?

Seasonal nightmare

For a Mumbaikar, every monsoon brings familiar scenes. Roads disappear beneath potholes. People lose their lives after falling into open manholes. Trees collapse. Ambulances remain trapped in endless traffic. Commuters spend hours covering distances that should take minutes. Meanwhile, those we elect often drive past under police escort, insulated from the inconvenience endured by the very citizens who voted them into office.

Some time ago, I spent nearly two-and-a-half hours travelling six kilometres on Mumbai’s Eastern Express Highway as a machine had fallen off a truck. The obstruction remained uncleared for hours. Ambulances stood trapped. Sirens echoed. Nothing moved. The incident made the news. The larger question didn’t.

Would the response have been equally slow had a Minister been caught in that traffic? Most citizens know the answer.

Watching Parliament today is often disheartening. Representatives entrusted with our mandate increasingly resemble participants in televised shouting contests rather than custodians of democracy.

The tragedy isn’t disagreement. Democracy requires disagreement. The tragedy is that disagreement has replaced governance. Many citizens voted hoping for balance. A government that governs. An Opposition that questions.

Instead, we often witness obstruction on one side and defensiveness on the other, while issues affecting ordinary citizens quietly slip off the national agenda.

Sadly, this behaviour has escaped Parliament and entered our everyday conversations. Television debates reward outrage. Social media rewards certainty. Listening has become weakness. Changing one’s opinion has become betrayal. We debate not to discover truth but to prove the other side wrong. But in business, organisations and families, we solve problems very differently.

We listen. We negotiate. We search for common ground. Why should politics be any different?

Ideological battlefields

Our political conversations increasingly resemble ideological battlefields. Capitalism creates wealth but often struggles with equitable distribution. Communism emphasises distribution while frequently weakening incentives for wealth creation. Must societies always choose between extremes? Or should mature democracies constantly search for better balance?

Progress has rarely emerged from absolute positions. It has emerged from thoughtful synthesis. Maybe politics deserves the same maturity.

The statistics tell an interesting story. A politically aware generation numbering tens of crores possesses the potential to reshape electoral outcomes, not necessarily by protesting, but simply by voting with purpose. Every political party should find that thought both inspiring and unsettling. Young Indians today are more connected, more informed and more vocal than any generation before them.

If they begin participating in elections with the same intensity with which they participate online, political calculations across the country could change dramatically. Not for the reason they support one ideology. But they begin demanding performance.

There is a catch

Every genuine public movement eventually attracts political attention. That is inevitable.

The challenge begins when a citizens’ movement slowly becomes indistinguishable from a political campaign.

The moment opposition parties openly embrace a movement, or the movement itself appears comfortable becoming an extension of partisan politics, the ordinary citizen begins asking uncomfortable questions.

Who is really benefiting from this anger?

That question does not invalidate the grievances. It simply reminds us that public trust depends on perceived independence.

A movement driven by students must continue to look like a movement driven by students. Otherwise, its moral authority weakens.

Accountability Is a two-way street

Governments cannot ignore genuine public anguish. Unaddressed frustration has a way of finding larger and less predictable expressions.

History repeatedly demonstrates that. But citizens, too, must confront uncomfortable truths. Paper leaks do not happen in isolation. They exist as someone leaks them. Someone purchases them. Someone profits from them. Corruption survives only when both supply and demand exist. We cannot condemn the system while quietly participating in it whenever it suits us.

It is no different from criticising corruption while attempting to bribe a traffic policeman after violating the law. Accountability cannot be selective.

The real lesson from the recent unrest is not about one examination, one movement or one political party. It is about the changing relationship between citizens and power. The youth of India may well become the country’s most effective accountability mechanism, not because they protest, but they refuse to be taken for granted.

Imagine a democracy where governments admit mistakes. Where oppositions contribute solutions instead of just amplifying outrage. Where debates generate ideas instead of insults. Where voters reward performance rather than permanent ideological loyalty.

That may sound idealistic. But then, every meaningful democratic reform once sounded unrealistic. Until it happened.

India’s greatest demographic dividend may not ultimately be its workforce. It may be its voters.

And the day India’s young citizens consistently vote with independence, question with fairness, protest with integrity and reject political capture, every political party, regardless of ideology, will finally understand that power belongs not to those who govern, but to those who grant the mandate.

A democracy was never designed to make governments comfortable. It was designed to make them accountable.

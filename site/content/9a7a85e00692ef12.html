COP31 failed to deliver. How climate negotiations can move forward from here

• INDIAN EXPRESS EXPLAINED • 2026-11-25

By Sameer Kwatra

Climate change is no longer a forecast. This year has brought catastrophic floods in Nepal that left thousands dead or missing, severe drought pushing 6.5 million people into acute hunger in Somalia, extreme heat across South Asia, a heat dome over North America, wildfires across Canada and a European summer where extreme heat claimed more than 50,000 lives.

Climate change is powerful and persistent; no country is immune, and the cost is not borne equally. And yet, even as the planet is smoldering, 31st Conference of the Parties (COP31) in Antalya, Turkey, demonstrated that the negotiations remain frozen in time. Despite being the longest COP to date, the process could not yield any agreement on either fossil fuels or finance.

To be fair, the multilateral COP framework has helped bend the curve on carbon emissions. Three decades ago, the world was hurtling toward 4 degrees Celsius of warming by the end of this century; today, projections point to roughly 2.8 degrees. Global diplomacy has also dragged climate change from the footnotes of national agendas into the spotlight.

Yet from COP1 in Berlin to COP31 in Antalya, the same fundamental questions remain unanswered: who pays and how much, who acts and how much?

Modest expectations

Expectations heading into Antalya were modest from the start. Framed as a technical summit focused on implementation rather than historic breakthroughs, COP31 took place in a world riven by geopolitical tensions, and the final COP31 decision mirrors that reality. The shortfalls are clearest on three fronts: fossil fuels, electrification and adaptation finance.

Even as the text admits for the first time that the world will likely overshoot the 1.5°C warming limit, (until the last push) it refused to name fossil fuels, the primary culprit. Earlier versions echoed the hard-won COP28 pledge to move away from fossil fuels and floated transition roadmaps; the failed final version retreated into vague language about shifting toward low-emissions, paired with contested references to carbon capture and removal.

Add to that, the Turkish presidency’s flagship push for 35% electrification by 2035 raised many questions about about what would power the electrification, will it lead to electrifying existing energy use or be subsumed in large growth of data centers, and finally who would pay for the immense investment needed for countries to electrify.

The missing ingredient is political will, not money. Wealthy nations cite constrained public budgets to justify stalling on climate finance, yet governments continue to direct hundreds of billions of dollars into fossil fuel subsidies, and risk expanding them in 2026 to cushion energy price spikes. Meanwhile, seven of the world’s largest oil and gas companies pocketed $150 billion in profits during the first nine months of the year.

A new playbook

Climate diplomacy needs a new playbook. Two practical shifts can get negotiations moving again: an integrated bargain that ties action to payment, and climate “minilateralism” in smaller coalitions.

The first is an integrated bargain. Rather than negotiating emissions cuts, finance for mitigation and adaptation, and technology in separate rooms, negotiators should move action, payment, and technology together. That comes down to four straightforward steps:

  1. Each country defines what needs to be done and what it actually costs;
  2. Wealthy nations agree on realistic contributions;
  3. Predictable funding and technology access are linked directly to measurable actions by developing nations;
  4. And timelines are staggered based on what each country can reasonably handle.

The second shift is climate “minilateralism”, an approach that has been discussed in various forms for the last several years. Think of this as “climate breakout rooms” where participants hash out thorny issues outside of the COP arena but report back to the full gathering. This would require smaller groupings such as the G20, BRICS and the Quad putting climate on their permanent agendas.

By hammering out practical deals on clean technology, resilient infrastructure, and early warning systems in smaller rooms, these coalitions can bring ready-made solutions back to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, so that COP shifts from an arena for endless positional posturing into a place where deals are actually finalised. Crucially, the COP process must stay inclusive to ensure that the most at-risk blocs, such as the Least Developed Countries and the Alliance of Small Island States, have a direct voice in shaping the outcomes.

Neither idea replaces the UN framework but gives it the engine it has been missing. Negotiators can soften draft texts and argue over baselines, but communities from Nepal to Europe cannot negotiate a delay with the next flood, drought or heatwave. Global warming is now a global warning.

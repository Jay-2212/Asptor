The minefield called footpath

By Vijaya Bharat • THE HINDU OPINION • 2026-08-24

My husband returned from his morning walk with a bruised knee. He had stumbled on a footpath. As he lay spreadeagled and shaken, two women looked at him from the corner of their eyes and walked past. More than the injury, he felt bad that the women had probably assumed that he had a drink too many and left him to his plight. Soon a few men rushed in and helped him to get up and offered to accompany him home.

He told me that the bruise on his knee was superficial but the hurt on his ego was deep: the episode was embarrassing and frightening. I didn’t want to rub salt on the wound by accusing him of walking carelessly and advise him to be mindful and to watch his steps. Glad that I didn’t lecture, because the very next day, I too fell on another footpath. The front part of my shoe got caught in a crevice and I fell face down. Though there was no bodily injury, I was concerned about my spectacles. One of the photochromatic progressive lenses fell from its frame. I too was fortunate not to sustain any injury and got up with embarrassment as people collected offering help.

Stunning blow

At 70-plus, my husband and I travel extensively and believe that physical fitness and mental alertness will make us enjoy the autumn of our lives. We became aware that such falls can break a bone or two, confine us to bed for long periods and lead to complications with collateral damage to other body systems, affecting recovery. Under such unfortunate circumstances, age will not be just a number but an important factor deciding recovery or otherwise.

When I advise my patients to walk and be active, many of them complain that there is no safe place to walk. Footpaths almost everywhere are dug, badly repaired, and dug again. While many modern houses are kept clean on the inside, construction debris and waste materials are thrown on the no-man’s land — the footpath.

In most places, the paver blocks are loose or broken and pathways resemble a corrugated cardboard with random dips and mounds in addition to heaps of dog poop and cow dung. In some otherwise good footpaths, overhanging plants, some thorny, make the pedestrians stoop or step on to the road. Moreover, it is not at all uncommon for hawkers to display their products and shops to extend into the footpath and for two-wheelers to be parked.

Considering all these deterrents, I started to walk on roads adjacent to the footpath. On a morning walk, a motorcyclist leaned sideways and tried to grab a gold chain from my neck. He didn’t succeed and I decided to avoid such threats by removing jewellery and keeping my mobile phone in the pocket of my track pants. One of my patients, also walking, advised me to face the incoming traffic to avoid getting hit from behind. I soon realised that any vehicle could come from any direction anytime. With no control on that factor, I carry a hiking stick to ward off stray dogs.

Despite all such threats, I continue to walk and wholeheartedly welcome the Supreme Court declaration that the right to walk safely on well-maintained footpaths is a Fundamental Right in the Constitution. On a recent trip to Taipei, Taiwan we were happy to experience what safe walking can feel like. My husband and I were excited and impressed with the green-coloured even footpaths with a painting of a man holding a child, even in small lanes. There were separate tracks for cycles and everywhere motor vehicles were parked on only one side of the lane, well within the marked areas. Traffic signals were working and rules were followed even when the traffic was light. At zebra crossings, there were clear displays indicating that pedestrians have the right of way.

All the above-mentioned events happened within a span of one month.

On our return flight, we hoped that the civil authorities will make the footpaths safe and the public will comply with a strong civic sense. The onus is on everyone.

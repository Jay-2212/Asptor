Saharanpur mosque demolition: The dispute and questions over haste

By Ishita Mishra • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-09-12

Bulldozers rolled into the Saharanpur collectorate compound before dawn on September 5, 2026. By the time the town woke up, a mosque that its management committee said had stood inside the campus for around 150 years, lay in ruins.

Six earth-movers carried out the demolition under a heavy security cordon that included Rapid Action Force (RAF) and police teams brought in from neighbouring districts.

The mosque had been on the administration’s radar for months. But the question that has grown bigger than the demolition itself is the speed with which the action was carried out.

The mosque committee lost its final legal appeal on September 2. The demolition followed roughly 48-60 hours after the ruling, leaving the committee with little time to approach a higher court.

The case

The controversy over the mosque started back in February 2025, when Vikas Tyagi, a Bajrang Dal leader, filed a complaint with the Saharanpur District Magistrate alleging that the mosque stood illegally on government land.

In his complaint, Mr. Tyagi claimed that the caretakers of the mosque were renting out rooms in the complex and collecting rent from a sub-post office operating there, and that they had no right to do so.

The complaint turned into a formal petition in March 2025, filed under the Public Premises Act before the city magistrate’s court.

The court proceedings

Before the city magistrate’s court, the mosque committee, led by Mohammad Tanvir Ahmed, submitted that the religious structure had existed at the site for around 150 years, well before the collectorate complex came into existence. The committee further contended that the land’s original owners, whom Mr. Ahmed identified as Yaqub Khan and Wahid Khan, had dedicated it as waqf property for use by worshippers.

The district administration, however, informed the court that the land on which the mosque stood had originally been meant for a rest house for people visiting the collectorate for business. It submitted that the land was subsequently used for a mosque without authorisation.

In his July 16, 2026, judgment, the magistrate said the mosque committee had failed to establish ownership of the land and held that the property in question was owned by the Uttar Pradesh Government.

The court held that the defendants had illegally occupied 315 square metres of government land and constructed a mosque on it without lawful authority, making the structure liable for eviction.

The court also considered it appropriate to calculate compensation from Fasli year 1359, corresponding to 1952 in the Gregorian calendar, following the abolition of the zamindari system. Based on the minimum circle rate of ₹58,200 per square metre and 70 years of alleged illegal occupation, the compensation was calculated at around ₹6.41 crore. The mosque management was given a 30 days to vacate the premises.

The mosque committee then moved the district court, which heard the matter through multiple hearings before dismissing the appeal on September 2. According to the mosque committee’s lawyer, it was given little time to approach the High Court. Within 48 hours of the district judge’s order, bulldozers brought down the mosque.

The political fallout

Soon after news of the demolition spread, political reactions followed.

Samajwadi Party chief Akhilesh Yadav attacked the U.P. government, led by Yogi Adityanath, and said the demolition of the 150-year-old mosque was a deliberate distraction from the BJP government’s alleged failures on jobs, inflation and unemployment.

He questioned why the State couldn’t wait for the matter to be taken to the High Court or the Supreme Court instead of tearing the building down within hours. He called it a “well-planned conspiracy,” and said the story had already made headlines beyond India, affecting the country’s image as a secular State.

All India Majlis-e-Ittehadul Muslimeen(AIMIM) chief Asaduddin Owaisi questioned why the entire action took place at 5 a.m. and asked what the haste was. He also wanted to know whether the constitutional right to religious freedom still meant anything for Muslims in India, and described the demolition as part of a “pattern of mosques being knocked down” on grounds he termed thin and manufactured.

The Congress said it would send a fact-finding team to Saharanpur and called the episode unfortunate. The State government’s line has stayed consistent: the action was never about religion but about an illegal structure on government land.

A well nobody expected

For now, the mosque is gone. But the legal battle has moved to the Allahabad High Court, which on Friday (September 11) ordered a stay on the recovery of the penalty imposed on the mosque management. The High Court also sought the UP government’s response to the petition challenging the action. The next hearing is scheduled for October 12.

Amid the political and legal fallout, something unexpected emerged as workers cleared the rubble: a structure buried about 20 feet below the surface. It was an old well, built with thin lakhori bricks, a type of brick commonly found in much older structures.

The discovery is notable because Mr. Tyagi had alleged in his original application that a temple may have stood on the site before the mosque was built.

Much like the disputes surrounding Gyanvapi and Mathura, the administration has now turned to archaeologists. They are examining the brickwork, mortar and layers of soil to determine how old the well might be and what it was originally used for.

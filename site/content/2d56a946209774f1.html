Why Delimitation Bill failed to pass in Parliament in April 2026

By The Hindu Bureau • THE HINDU NATIONAL • 2026-11-27

A special parliamentary sitting has been announced on November 30, 2026, bringing renewed focus to the delimitation debate after more than 100 days without prorogation following the August 13 monsoon adjournment.

The Union government is set to bring an amended Delimitation Bill that proposes a uniform 50% increase in Lok Sabha seats, extends the 1971 Census freeze on state seat shares for 25 years and uses the 2011 Census to redraw constituencies within each state.

The legislation failed to secure the required two-thirds majority in the Lok Sabha when it was put to a vote in April 2026, with the opposition citing concerns about the government’s move to link women’s reservation with delimitation based on the 2011 Census. They also pointed out the absence of a clear guarantee that the representation of all States in the Lok Sabha would increase uniformly by 50%, as the government had claimed.

What is Delimitation?

Delimitation is the process of fixing the number of seats and boundaries of the territorial constituencies in each State for the Lok Sabha and Legislative Assemblies. This ‘Delimitation process’ is performed by a higher-power body called the ‘Delimitation Commission’ that is set up under an act of Parliament. Such exercises have previously been conducted based on the 1951, 1961, and 1971 Censuses. The last time the number of seats was determined was on the basis of 1971 census population.

The current Delimitation Bill aims to significantly expand the Lok Sabha from 543 to 850 seats (815 from States, 35 from Union Territories), acknowledging the substantial population growth in India based on the latest census figures. This bill will officially end the 50-year “freeze” on reallocating seats between States, allowing for a redistribution based on current population trends. Opposition have voiced concern that the exercise could have significant consequences for political power and influence at both the State and National levels.

Why the Amendment fell short in April 2026?

In April, the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill failed to win two-thirds of the majority to amend the Constitution. Out of 528 members present and voting in the Lok Sabha, 298 voted in favour and 230 voted against, falling short of the 352 votes needed.

Because the constitutional amendment did not pass, the government shelved the companion Delimitation Bill and Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, stating they could not be viewed in isolation

Federal concerns

A major concern back in April was how seats would be allocated across states. The tabled text linked delimitation to the latest published census, which was the 2011 census, rather than writing a guaranteed proportional seat share into the bill. Following through with it would have reduced the share of southern, eastern, and northeastern states due to their lower population growth relative to the Hindi heartland states.

Home Minister Amit Shah offered a verbal assurance of a 50% pro rata increase and floated redrafting it, but the opposition rejected this since the written text pointed elsewhere.

Opposition parties also objected to tying one-third reservation for women to delimitation and rushing the package through Parliament. Under the 106th Constitutional Amendment passed in 2023, women’s reservation was linked to delimitation following the next census, whereas the 131st Amendment proposed raising the Lok Sabha ceiling from 550 to 850 and linking women’s quota implementation to delimitation based on the 2011 census.

How the numbers have shifted since April

On the numbers side, shifts in Lok Sabha, including 20 TMC MPs moving to the Nationalist Citizens Party of India (NCPI), six Shiv Sena MPs joining the Shinde faction and reported backing from the SAD MP and four YSR Congress MPs have changed the stakes.

Assuming full attendance in the 543-member Lok Sabha, the government requires 362 votes. There are three vacancies in the Lok Sabha right now, so the two-thirds threshold comes down to 360 votes.

A senior NDA leader told The Hindu that the government is confident of the support of at least 358 MPs, just two short of the required strength if all 540 MPs are present and voting.

Prominent leaders, including voices within the DMK (with 22 Lok Sabha MPs), are weighing the risk of state seat losses if the current amendment is not passed, pressing for fair delimitation. DMK president M.K. Stalin on Friday (November 27, 2026) made it clear that there is no change in the position of his party and no question of any compromise on the issue of Delimitation.

“The DMK stands firm on its two demands that if the number of parliamentary constituencies is increased, under no circumstances should Tamil Nadu’s existing 7.18% share of seats in the lower house be reduced, and the existing proportion of parliamentary representation among States, as determined on the basis of the 1971 Census and frozen earlier, must remain frozen for another 25 years,” Mr. Stalin said in a post on X, brushing aside speculations about the party’s stand on delimitation.

Meanwhile, Senior NCP (SP) leader Supriya Sule, on Thursday (November 26), told reporters that pre-emptively opposing the law before its draft was made public was not a good move.

“First let the bill come. Let the entire bill come into our hands. When the bill is in our hands, then we will all talk about it, and if the bill is good for the nation, we might support it,” she said. She further added that there have been instances in the past when the Opposition has supported the government on matters of national importance and key reforms. NCP currently has eight Lok Sabha MPs.

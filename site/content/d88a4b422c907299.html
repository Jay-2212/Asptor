NEET and the state’s duty to minimise harm

• THE HINDU OPINION • 2026-08-06

The past year marked an important shift in the constitutional understanding of student well-being. In Sukdeb Saha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2025), the Supreme Court recognised student mental health as part of the right to life under Article 21 and treated student suicides as failures that can arise from institutional conditions. The court directed educational institutions to adopt mental health policies and create psychologically safe environments for students. More importantly, it shifted the focus to the role of educational institutions in protecting student well-being. That shift was reinforced in Amit Kumar v. Union of India (2026), in which the court warned against the tendency to individualise student suicides while ignoring institutionally normalised stressors.

The Supreme Court-appointed National Task Force, in its interim report, similarly located student distress in a range of institutional, academic, social, and economic pressures. The NEET paper leak has become one of the earliest tests of the court’s recent recognition of institutional responsibility for student well-being. It raises a question about whether constitutional principles can reshape the exercise of public power.

The NEET controversy presented precisely the kind of situation that the court’s recent jurisprudence sought to address. Even before the police resorted to disproportionate force and surveillance against protesting students, the paper leak and the events that followed had already exposed over 20 lakh students to exactly the kind of pressures identified in Sukdeb Saha and by the National Task Force.

The NEET controversy acquired national attention because its consequences were immediate and visible. Yet the constitutional significance of institutional failure cannot depend upon the scale of public outrage it generates. Across the country, repeated failures in examination and recruitment processes routinely subject students and job-seekers to uncertainty with far less public scrutiny. Pratap Bhanu Mehta recently captured this culture through the ironic description of students as “Exam Warriors”. The metaphor is revealing because wars are expected to demand endurance and produce casualties. However, the understanding reflected in Sukdeb Saha rests on a different premise.

It is important to note that Sukdeb Saha was decided in the context of educational institutions. But the right to mental health cannot exclude aspirants for public employment in their interactions with the state. If educational institutions exercising decisive control over educational opportunities have a duty to minimise foreseeable harms, it is difficult to explain why similar expectations should not inform public institutions that exercise comparable control over access to public employment. Recruitment examinations rarely generate the same public urgency as entrance examinations because their consequences unfold gradually rather than through a single admission cycle. Yet delays, irregular notifications, prolonged vacancies and unpredictable recruitment processes often leave aspirants suspended in years of uncertainty.

This is significant because access to public employment, especially through reservation policies, often places aspirants in a relationship in which the state is understood as conferring a favour rather than discharging a constitutional obligation. In this unequal relationship, aspirants have very little bargaining power. The recent film Homebound captures this reality. A large section of society remains dependent on opportunities created by the state, but the path to securing them has become so uncertain that only the privileged can often afford to remain in the race.

The state exercises decisive control over access to higher education and public employment. That control carries not only a negative duty to avoid arbitrary decision-making but also a positive duty to create fair systems that minimise foreseeable institutional harm. The events of 2026 were possible because the lessons of the 2024 NEET controversy were never learnt. If the state’s response is confined to resolving individual controversies without undertaking structural reform, the same crises will continue to recur.

The understanding reflected in Sukdeb Saha must also inform the manner in which courts approach institutional measures intended to create safer educational environments. The Supreme Court’s interim suspension of the UGC Equity Regulations must also be assessed in light of its implications for the institutional conditions that shape student well-being. Similarly, the initial judicial responses of the Supreme Court and the Delhi High Court to challenges arising from the treatment of protesting students illustrate how easily institutional concerns can be viewed through the narrower lens of public order or administrative convenience.

If Sukdeb Saha is to represent a genuine constitutional shift, its principle cannot remain confined to educational institutions. It must shape the exercise of public power wherever the state exercises decisive control over educational or professional futures. Otherwise, institutional responsibility will remain a constitutional promise recognised in judgments while avoidable uncertainty continues to shape the lives of millions of students and aspirants.

Shantanu Pachauri is an Assistant Professor and Associate Dean, Student Affairs, School of Law, RV University, Bengaluru

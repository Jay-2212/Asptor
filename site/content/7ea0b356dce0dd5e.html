Tata Steel’s APL Apollo deal: Why investors are worried about the Rs 28,000-crore bet

Tata Steel on Wednesday (November 25) said it was reviewing the structure of its proposed Rs 28,000-crore deal with APL Apollo Tubes, following investor backlash. The concerns centre on the deal’s valuation, how it will be financed, the risk of dilution for shareholders and commercial arrangements involving a promoter-linked entity.

After a sharp fall of over 21% on Monday, APL Apollo shares have fallen another 11.4% on Wednesday. Tata Steel’s shares also declined 2.54%, after the 8% fall on Monday, in a strong market following concerns over the cost of the acquisition and how the company intends to fund it.

While the strategic rationale is clear, the financial implications for shareholders, who were flummoxed by the two-leg transaction, remain less so. There may be a case for expansion, but the price it pays and the manner in which it finances the transaction ultimately determine whether shareholders benefit.

Why is the market uneasy?

On Sunday, Tata Steel announced plans to sell its entire steel tubes business and its wholly owned subsidiary, Tata Steel Colors Pvt Ltd, to APL Apollo Tubes for a combined consideration of Rs 28,000 crore. A day later, Tata Steel said it will acquire a 26.6% stake in APL Apollo Tubes from its promoters – Sanjay Gupta and family – for Rs 15,510 crore and make an open offer for an additional 26 per cent stake.

Effectively, APL Apollo would borrow Rs 28,000 crore to buy two Tata businesses and Tata Steel, in turn, would use that money to buy out APL Apollo’s Guptas. Investors are debating whether minority shareholders will approve the transfer of two business units as promoters – mostly Tata Sons – hold only 32.94% in Tata Sons.

Even as Tata Steel MD TV Narendran on Wednesday indicated that it will review the acquisition structure, the market is apparently worried about the valuation of the transaction, financing structure and the impact on minority shareholders.

The approximately Rs 28,000-crore valuation attributed to the tubes and colour-coated businesses has drawn scrutiny, particularly in comparison with the historical net block of the assets being transferred. Questions have also surfaced over the valuation of Tata Steel Colors following the buyout of its joint venture partner, BlueScope.

In a conference call on Monday, Tata Steel CFO Koushik Chatterjee has defended the valuation, arguing that historical asset values do not adequately capture the growth prospects, distribution advantages and operational synergies of the businesses. While businesses are valued on their future cash flows rather than their asset costs, the assumptions underpinning those projections matter.

“Investors need greater clarity on expected revenue growth, operating margins, capital expenditure and the return on invested capital,” said an investment analyst. “The valuation will ultimately have to be justified through earnings and cash flows. Projected synergies, however attractive on paper, cannot be treated as realised returns.”

Investors are also weighing execution risks, leverage, integration challenges and the time required for the acquisition to contribute meaningfully to earnings.

Financing the deal, risk of dilution

The financing structure is a source of concern for investors. Of the approximately Rs 28,000-crore consideration, around Rs 10,000 crore is expected to remain as external debt, with the balance to be addressed through equity or other capitalisation instruments. The management has indicated that APL Apollo’s net debt-to-EBITDA ratio is expected to decline to approximately 2.5 times within 12 months of closing. The route to achieving this target, however, will be important for shareholders.

“Fresh equity issuance at an unfavourable valuation could dilute existing shareholders, while higher debt could increase interest costs and constrain cash flows. The financing structure is therefore central to the transaction, not merely a technical detail to be settled after the acquisition,” said a fund manager tracking the deal.

Chatterjee has said the instruments will be structured fairly and will not prejudice existing shareholders. Tata Steel has also indicated that it will not vote on the relevant resolution at APL Apollo’s shareholder meeting, leaving continuing shareholders to decide on the material transaction. The assurances will need to be backed by transparent terms, including the pricing and nature of the instruments and their implications for existing shareholders, analysts assert.

Narendran said APL Apollo’s established brand, distribution network and customer relationships, built over 15-20 years, would be difficult and time-consuming for Tata Steel to replicate organically. While the acquisition offers faster market access and complements Tata Steel’s downstream business, the key question is whether the premium paid will generate adequate returns or erode shareholder value if expected synergies take longer to materialise.

SG Group arrangement raises governance questions

The proposed exclusive contract-manufacturing arrangement with SG Tech Engineering, a private entity associated with APL Apollo’s promoter group, adds another dimension to the transaction. Contract manufacturing can help companies expand without committing substantial capital to new facilities.

Narendran has argued that the arrangement will allow Tata Steel to grow its colour-coated products business without investing directly in additional manufacturing capacity.

However, a commercial arrangement involving a promoter-linked entity warrants closer scrutiny, particularly when shareholders are evaluating a transaction of this scale. Pricing, operating costs, capital expenditure, capacity commitments, contractual duration and the allocation of risks need to be clearly disclosed. Investors must be able to assess whether the arrangement is commercially beneficial to the listed entity.

Management has said that transactions between Tata Steel and APL Apollo will be conducted at market rates and on an arm’s-length basis.

For Tata Steel shareholders, the acquisition raises the question of whether the capital committed to the transaction could generate better returns through strengthening existing operations, reducing debt or pursuing organic expansion. APL Apollo’s continuing public shareholders, meanwhile, will have to assess whether the proposed recapitalisation and associated commercial arrangements distribute risks and rewards fairly.
